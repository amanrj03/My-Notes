📘 MODULE 1 (Detailed): Foundations of Public Key Cryptography

1. The Problem with Classical Cryptography (Why Public Key?)

In Unit IV, you studied Private Key Cryptography (like the Shift or Affine cipher).

  • The Rule: The encryption key and decryption key are mathematically linked. If you know \(k\), you can easily find \(k^{-1}\).
  • The Flaw: If Alice wants to send a secret to Bob, she needs the key. But to get the key to Bob securely, she must send it over a channel. If a hacker intercepts the key, the entire system is broken.

The Public Key Solution (Diffie & Hellman, 1976): What if the encryption key and decryption key are different?

  • Everyone has a Public Key (\(E\)). You can shout it to the world.
  • Everyone has a Private Key (\(D\)). You keep it locked in a safe.
  • Anyone can use your Public Key to encrypt a message, but only you can use your Private Key to decrypt it.

Mathematically, this requires a Trapdoor One-Way Function:

  • \(f(x) = y\) is easy to compute.
  • \(f^{-1}(y) = x\) is practically impossible to compute... UNLESS you have a secret piece of information (the trapdoor).

2. The Number Theory Tools (The Engine of Unit V)

One-Way Trapdoor Functions & Fast Modular Exponentiation

🚪 The Trapdoor Mechanism
Easy: Computing \(y = f(x)\) in polynomial time \(O(\log^3 n)\).
Hard: Finding \(x\) given \(y\) without special knowledge (infeasible).
Trapdoor: With private key \(K_{priv}\), inverting \(x = f^{-1}(y)\) becomes computationally easy.
⚡ Fast Repeated Squaring
To calculate \(a^b \bmod n\):
Convert exponent \(b\) into binary: \(b = \sum b_i 2^i\).
Square intermediate results at each step and multiply by \(a\) only when bit \(b_i = 1\).
Reduces \(O(b)\) naive multiplications to \(O(\log b)\)!

To understand RSA and ElGamal (Modules 3 and 4), you must be a master of these four mathematical tools.

Tool 1 & 2: Extended Euclidean Algorithm & Modular Inverse Engine

GUARANTEED EXAM TOPIC

The mathematical heart of RSA key derivation: Computing \(\gcd(a, b)\), expressing it as a linear combination \(ax + by = \gcd(a, b)\) (Bézout's Identity), and deriving the modular multiplicative inverse \(a^{-1} \pmod m\).

1 Worked Exam Example: Find \(\gcd(26, 7)\) and Express as \(26x + 7y\)
↓ Step 1: Forward Euclidean Divisions
Step Division Equation Remainder
Div 1 \(26 = 3 imes 7 + 5\) \(r_1 = 5\)
Div 2 \(7 = 1 imes 5 + 2\) \(r_2 = 2\)
Div 3 \(5 = 2 imes 2 + 1\) \(r_3 = 1\) (GCD)
Div 4 \(2 = 2 imes 1 + 0\) \(r_4 = 0\) (Stop)
✓ Last non-zero remainder is 1 → \(\gcd(26, 7) = 1\) (Coprime).
↑ Step 2: Backward Substitution (Bézout Form)
Isolate GCD (1) from Div 3:
\(1 = 5 - 2 imes 2\)
Substitute \(2 = 7 - 1 imes 5\) from Div 2:
\(1 = 5 - 2 imes (7 - 1 imes 5) = 3 imes 5 - 2 imes 7\)
Substitute \(5 = 26 - 3 imes 7\) from Div 1:
\(1 = 3 imes (26 - 3 imes 7) - 2 imes 7 = 3 imes 26 - 11 imes 7\)
Bézout Form: \(26(3) + 7(-11) = 1\) • \(x = 3, \; y = -11\)
2 Tool 2: Deriving the Modular Multiplicative Inverse
Rule: Inverse exists ⇔ \(\gcd(a, m) = 1\)

The inverse of \(a \pmod m\) is an integer \(a^{-1}\) satisfying \(a \cdot a^{-1} \equiv 1 \pmod m\). From Bézout's identity \(ax + my = 1\), reducing modulo \(m\) gives:
\(ax + my \equiv 1 \pmod m \implies ax + 0 \equiv 1 \pmod m \implies a \cdot x \equiv 1 \pmod m\) Therefore, the Bézout coefficient \(x\) is the modular multiplicative inverse of \(a \pmod m\).

Step 1: Extract Coefficient
From our example: \(3 imes 26 - 11 imes 7 = 1\).
Modulo 26: \((-11 imes 7) \equiv 1 \pmod{26}\).
Raw modular inverse of \(7 \pmod{26}\) is \(-11\).
Step 2: Convert to Positive Standard Range
Add modulus \(26\) to make it positive in range \(\{0, 1, \dots, 25\}\):
\(-11 + 26 = 15 \pmod{26}\)
Check: \(7 imes 15 = 105 = 4 imes 26 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{26}\). Correct!

Tool 3: Euler’s Totient Function, \(\phi(n)\)

\(\phi(n)\) is the count of integers from \(1\) to \(n\) that are relatively prime to \(n\) (i.e., \(\gcd(k, n) = 1\)).

  • If \(n\) is prime (\(p\)), then all numbers from \(1\) to \(p-1\) are coprime to it. So, \(\phi(p) = p - 1\).
  • If \(n = p \times q\) (where \(p\) and \(q\) are distinct primes), we use the formula:
\[\phi(n) = \phi(p) \times \phi(q) = (p - 1)(q - 1)\]

Tool 4: Fermat’s Little Theorem & Euler’s Theorem

These theorems are the reason RSA decryption actually works.

  • Fermat’s Little Theorem: If \(p\) is prime and \(\gcd(a, p) = 1\), then:
\[a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p\]
  • Euler’s Theorem (Generalization): If \(\gcd(a, n) = 1\), then:
\[a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n\]

Fast Modular Exponentiation: Step-by-Step Repeated Squaring Walkthrough

CRUCIAL EXAM TECHNIQUE

Calculating large powers like \(a^b mod n\) by hand without a calculator: Decompose the exponent into binary powers of 2 and square modulo \(n\) successively.

• Worked Example: Calculate \(2^{13} mod 11\)
1. Binary Exponent Decomposition
Write exponent \(13\) as sum of powers of 2:
\(13 = 8 + 4 + 1 = (1101)_2\) Therefore, by exponent addition laws:
\(2^{13} = 2^8 \cdot 2^4 \cdot 2^1 \pmod{11}\)
2. Successive Squaring Table (mod 11)
Power Calculation Result (mod 11) Status
\(2^1\) Base value \(2\) Included (bit=1)
\(2^2\) \(2^2 = 4\) \(4\) Skipped (bit=0)
\(2^4\) \(4^2 = 16 = 11 + 5\) \(5\) Included (bit=1)
\(2^8\) \(5^2 = 25 = 2(11) + 3\) \(3\) Included (bit=1)
3. Multiply Included Powers: \(2^{13} = 2^8 \cdot 2^4 \cdot 2^1 \equiv 3 imes 5 imes 2 = 30 \equiv 8 \pmod{11}\)
Answer: \(2^{13} \equiv 8 \pmod{11}\) ✓

Q1. (MCQ — 1 Mark) Totient Calculation

If \(n = 77\), what is \(\phi(n)\)?

A) 76
B) 60 ✓ (Correct Option)
C) 70
D) 66
Step-by-Step Derivation:
• Step 1: Factor \(77\) into prime factors: \(77 = 7 imes 11\)
• Step 2: Apply Euler's product formula for distinct primes: \(\phi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)\)
• Step 3: Compute: \(\phi(77) = (7-1)(11-1) = 6 imes 10 = \mathbf{60}\)
⇒ Answer: B
Q2. (Solving — 5 Marks) Extended Euclidean Algorithm

Find the modular inverse of \(13 \pmod{42}\) using the Extended Euclidean Algorithm.

Step 1: Forward Euclidean Divisions on 42 and 13:
1. \(42 = 3 imes 13 + 3\)   •   (Remainder = 3)
2. \(13 = 4 imes 3 + 1\)   •   (Remainder = 1)
3. \(3 = 3 imes 1 + 0\)   •   (Remainder = 0 → \(\gcd(42, 13) = 1\), so inverse exists)
Step 2: Backward Substitution (Isolate Remainder 1):
• From division 2: \(1 = 13 - 4 imes 3\)
• Substitute \(3 = 42 - 3 imes 13\) from division 1:
   \(1 = 13 - 4 imes (42 - 3 imes 13)\)
   \(1 = 13 - 4 imes 42 + 12 imes 13\)
   \(1 = 13 imes 13 - 4 imes 42\)
Step 3: Read Coefficient Modulo 42:
Reducing modulo 42 eliminates the multiple of 42:
\(13 imes 13 \equiv 1 \pmod{42}\)
⇒ The modular inverse of \(13 \pmod{42}\) is \(\mathbf{13}\).
Check: \(13 imes 13 = 169 = 4 imes 42 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{42}\). (Exact match!)
Q3. (Solving — 4 Marks) FLT & Exponentiation

Calculate \(3^{17} mod 5\) using Fermat's Little Theorem and modular exponentiation.

Method 1: Fermat's Little Theorem (Fastest)
• Since \(p = 5\) is prime and \(\gcd(3, 5) = 1\):
   \(3^{5-1} = 3^4 \equiv 1 \pmod 5\)
• Decompose exponent \(17\): \(17 = 4 imes 4 + 1\)
• Substitute:
   \(3^{17} = (3^4)^4 imes 3^1 \equiv 1^4 imes 3 \equiv \mathbf{3} \pmod 5\)
Method 2: Binary Exponentiation (Step-by-Step)
• Binary for 17: \(17 = 16 + 1\)
• \(3^1 = 3\)
• \(3^2 = 9 \equiv 4 \equiv -1 \pmod 5\)
• \(3^4 = (-1)^2 = 1\)
• \(3^8 = 1^2 = 1\)
• \(3^{16} = 1^2 = 1\)
• Product: \(3^{17} = 3^{16} \cdot 3^1 = 1 \cdot 3 = \mathbf{3} \pmod 5\)
⇒ Final Answer: \(3^{17} \equiv 3 \pmod 5\) ✓

1. What mathematical construct forms the foundational requirement for public key cryptography?
Mathematical Explanation: Public key cryptography is founded upon one-way trapdoor functions that are computationally easy to evaluate forward but infeasible to invert without the secret trapdoor.
2. What is the time complexity of naive modular exponentiation a^b mod n compared to repeated squaring?
Mathematical Explanation: Fast modular exponentiation (repeated squaring) evaluates a^b mod n in O(log b) multiplications, whereas naive multiplication requires O(b).
3. For any prime integer p, what is Euler's totient function φ(p)?
Mathematical Explanation: For any prime p, Euler's totient function is φ(p) = p - 1.
4. If p = 5 and q = 11 are distinct primes, what is φ(pq)?
Mathematical Explanation: Since 5 and 11 are prime, φ(55) = (5 - 1)(11 - 1) = 4 × 10 = 40.
5. Under what condition does an integer a possess a modular multiplicative inverse modulo m?
Mathematical Explanation: A modular multiplicative inverse of a modulo m exists if and only if gcd(a, m) = 1.
6. What is the modular multiplicative inverse of 3 modulo 11?
Mathematical Explanation: 3 × 4 = 12 ≡ 1 (mod 11), so the modular inverse of 3 modulo 11 is 4.
7. Compute 3¹⁷ mod 5 using Fermat's Little Theorem.
Mathematical Explanation: By Fermat's Little Theorem, 3⁴ ≡ 1 (mod 5). 3¹⁷ = (3⁴)⁴ · 3¹ ≡ 1⁴ · 3 ≡ 3 (mod 5).
8. How many pairwise secret keys are required for n users in symmetric private-key cryptography?
Mathematical Explanation: For n users to communicate in symmetric pairs, n(n - 1)/2 shared keys are needed, creating an O(n²) key distribution bottleneck.
