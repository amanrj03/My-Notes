Welcome to Module 3: The RSA Cryptosystem.

This is the most important module in Unit V. In almost every exam that covers Public Key Cryptography, there will be a 10-mark numerical question asking you to either generate RSA keys, encrypt a message, or decrypt a ciphertext.

We will break this down mathematically, exactly as your textbook (Pages 6-8) does.

📘 MODULE 3: The RSA Cryptosystem

1. The Mathematical Foundation (The Trapdoor)

RSA was invented by Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman in 1977. It relies on the fact that:

  • Easy: Multiplying two large primes \(p\) and \(q\) to get \(n = p \times q\).
  • Hard: Factoring \(n\) back into \(p\) and \(q\) (if \(p\) and \(q\) are 200 digits each, it takes billions of years).

Because of this, the public key can include \(n\), but only the person who knows the factors \(p\) and \(q\) can find the private decryption key.

2. RSA Key Generation (Step-by-Step)

The RSA Cryptosystem: 5-Stage Mathematical Master Pipeline

1
Choose Two Large Primes
Select distinct primes \(p\) and \(q\). In production, these are 1024 to 2048 bits long.
2
Calculate Modulus \(n\) and Euler's Totient \(\phi(n)\)
\(n = p \cdot q\) • \(\phi(n) = (p - 1)(q - 1)\). Modulus \(n\) is made public; \(\phi(n)\) is strictly secret!
3
Select Public Exponent \(e\)
Choose \(e\) such that \(1 < e < \phi(n)\) and \(\gcd(e, \phi(n)) = 1\) (common choices: 3, 17, 65537).
4
Derive Private Exponent \(d\) via Extended Euclidean Algorithm
Compute modular inverse: \(d \equiv e^{-1} \pmod{\phi(n)}\), meaning \(ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\phi(n)}\).
5
Encryption & Decryption Execution
Public Key: \((n, e)\) • Private Key: \(d\)
Encryption: \(c \equiv m^e \pmod n\) • Decryption: \(m \equiv c^d \pmod n\)

To create a public and private key, follow these exact steps:

  1. Choose two distinct large primes, \(p\) and \(q\).

  2. Compute the modulus: \(n = p \times q\). (Note: The public key will be \((n, e)\)).

  3. Compute Euler’s Totient: \(\phi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)\).

  4. Choose a public exponent \(e\): Choose an integer \(e\) such that \(1 \lt e \lt \phi(n)\) and \(\gcd(e, \phi(n)) = 1\). (This means \(e\) and \(\phi(n)\) share no common factors).
  5. Compute the private exponent \(d\): Find the modular inverse of \(e\) modulo \(\phi(n)\). This means solving: \(e \cdot d \equiv 1 \pmod{\phi(n)}\). (You use the Extended Euclidean Algorithm from Module 1 for this). (Note: The private key is \(d\) or \((n, d)\)).

Public Key: \((n, e)\) Private Key: \(d\) (or \((n, d)\))

3. RSA Encryption

To encrypt a message, we convert the text to numbers (A=00, B=01, ..., Z=25). We split the numbers into blocks \(M\) such that \(M \lt n\). The encryption formula is:

\[C = M^e \bmod n\]

(You use Fast Modular Exponentiation to calculate this).

4. RSA Decryption

To decrypt the ciphertext \(C\), we use the private key \(d\):

\[M = C^d \bmod n\]

(Again, use Fast Modular Exponentiation).

📝 Solved Exam Questions

Here is how RSA is tested in exams.

Question 1 (Key Generation - 5 Marks): Let \(p = 3\) and \(q = 11\). Choose \(e = 7\). Find the public key, private key, and \(\phi(n)\). Solution:

  1. \(n = p \times q = 3 \times 11 = 33\).
  2. \(\phi(n) = (3-1)(11-1) = 2 \times 10 = 20\).
  3. Check \(\gcd(7, 20) = 1\). (Yes, 7 and 20 share no factors).
  4. Public Key: \((n, e) = (33, 7)\).
  5. Find \(d\) such that \(7d \equiv 1 \pmod{20}\). Test values: \(7(3) = 21 \equiv 1 \pmod{20}\). So, \(d = 3\). Private Key: \(d = 3\).

Question 2 (Full RSA Calculation — 10 Marks): "STOP" Encryption & "HELP" Decryption

HIGHEST YIELD EXAM PROBLEM

Classic university examination question: Given primes \(p = 43\), \(q = 59\), and public exponent \(e = 13\). (a) Encrypt plaintext "STOP", (b) Decrypt ciphertext "0981 0461".

1. System Setup & Parameter Derivation
Modulus \(n\):
\(n = p imes q = 43 imes 59 = \mathbf{2537}\)
Euler's Totient \(\phi(n)\):
\(\phi(n) = (42)(58) = \mathbf{2436}\)
Public Key:
\((n, e) = \mathbf{(2537, 13)}\)
2. Part (a): Encrypting "STOP" (Block Size Constraint)

Convert letters to 2-digit numbers: S=18, T=19, O=14, P=15 → String 1819 1415. Since \(2525 < n (2537) < 252525\), plaintext must be partitioned into 4-digit blocks so each \(M_i < n\).

Block 1: \(M_1 = 1819\)
\(C_1 = M_1^e mod n = 1819^{13} mod 2537\)
\(C_1 = 2081\)
Block 2: \(M_2 = 1415\)
\(C_2 = M_2^e mod n = 1415^{13} mod 2537\)
\(C_2 = 2182\)
Transmitted Ciphertext: 2081 2182
3. Part (b): Decrypting "0981 0461"

First compute private key \(d\) by solving \(13d \equiv 1 \pmod{2436}\) using the Extended Euclidean Algorithm:
\(d = 937\) • Check: \(13 imes 937 = 12181 = 5 imes 2436 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{2436}\).

Block 1: \(C_1 = 981\)
\(M_1 = 981^{937} mod 2537 = \mathbf{704} o 0704\)
Characters: 07 = H, 04 = E
Block 2: \(C_2 = 461\)
\(M_2 = 461^{937} mod 2537 = \mathbf{1115}\)
Characters: 11 = L, 15 = P
Decrypted Plaintext: HELP ✓

(Note for Exams: In a 10-mark question, you will not be expected to do \(981^{937}\) by hand. The question will either give you the answer, or the numbers will be small enough to use tricks. The crucial part they grade is your setup and your decryption formula.)

Question 3 (Theoretical/Proof - 5 Marks): Why does RSA Decryption work? Prove that \((M^e)^d \equiv M \pmod n\). Solution: By definition, \(e \cdot d \equiv 1 \pmod{\phi(n)}\). This means there exists an integer \(k\) such that \(ed = 1 + k(p-1)(q-1)\). Therefore: \(C^d \equiv (M^e)^d = M^{ed} = M^{1 + k(p-1)(q-1)} = M \cdot (M^{p-1})^{k(q-1)} \pmod n\). By Fermat's Little Theorem (since \(p\) is prime), \(M^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p\). So, modulo \(p\), \(C^d \equiv M \cdot 1 = M \pmod p\). Similarly, modulo \(q\), \(C^d \equiv M \pmod q\). By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, since \(C^d \equiv M \pmod p\) and \(C^d \equiv M \pmod q\), it must be true that \(C^d \equiv M \pmod{pq}\), which is \(n\). Thus, \(M = C^d \bmod n\). Q.E.D.

5. Why is RSA Secure? (For MCQs)

An attacker knows the public key \((n, e)\). To find the private key \(d\), the attacker needs \(\phi(n)\). To find \(\phi(n)\), the attacker must know \(p\) and \(q\). To find \(p\) and \(q\), the attacker must factor \(n\). Since factoring large \(n\) is computationally infeasible, RSA is secure.

  3. Decrypt: Apply \(M = C^d \bmod n\) and convert numbers back to text.

1. The cryptographic security of RSA is primarily founded upon the hardness of:
Mathematical Explanation: The security of RSA is founded on the computational hardness of factoring large composite integers n = pq.
2. If p = 3 and q = 11, what are the modulus n and Euler's totient φ(n)?
Mathematical Explanation: Modulus n = 3 × 11 = 33, and Euler's totient φ(n) = (3 - 1)(11 - 1) = 2 × 10 = 20.
3. Which condition must the public encryption exponent e satisfy in RSA?
Mathematical Explanation: The public exponent e must satisfy 1 < e < φ(n) and gcd(e, φ(n)) = 1.
4. Given φ(n) = 20 and public exponent e = 3, what is the private decryption key d?
Mathematical Explanation: d is the modular inverse of e modulo φ(n): 3d ≡ 1 (mod 20). 3 × 7 = 21 ≡ 1 (mod 20), so d = 7.
5. What is the encryption formula in RSA for message m with public key (n, e)?
Mathematical Explanation: RSA encryption computes ciphertext c ≡ m^e (mod n).
6. What is the decryption formula in RSA for ciphertext c with private key d?
Mathematical Explanation: RSA decryption computes plaintext m ≡ c^d (mod n).
7. If n = 33 and e = 3, encrypt the plaintext message m = 2.
Mathematical Explanation: c = 2³ mod 33 = 8.
8. Why is standard textbook RSA described as a deterministic cryptosystem?
Mathematical Explanation: RSA is deterministic because the same message m encrypted with key (n, e) always yields the identical ciphertext c.
9. Which mathematical theorem provides the proof of correctness for RSA decryption m^(ed) ≡ m (mod n)?
Mathematical Explanation: RSA correctness is proven using Euler's Totient Theorem: m^{φ(n)} ≡ 1 (mod n) when gcd(m, n) = 1, and CRT.
10. If an attacker can factor modulus n into primes p and q, how is the system compromised?
Mathematical Explanation: Factoring n yields p and q, allowing immediate calculation of φ(n) and the private key d ≡ e⁻¹ mod φ(n).
