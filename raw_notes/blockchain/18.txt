Topic 1: Introduction to Bitcoin Investment
1.1 What is Bitcoin investment?
First, understand investment through a simple example.

Suppose Aman purchases gold worth ₹50,000.

He believes that the price of gold may increase in the future.

After some time, if the price increases, he may sell the gold at a higher price and earn a profit.

The same basic idea applies to Bitcoin investment.

Bitcoin investment means purchasing Bitcoin with the expectation that its value may increase in the future, allowing the investor to sell it at a profit.

For example:
- Aman purchases Bitcoin worth ₹10,000.
- After some time, its value increases to ₹12,000.
- He sells his Bitcoin.
His profit is:
\[
Profit = Selling\ Price - Buying\ Price
\]

\[
= ₹12,000-₹10,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Profit=₹2,000}
\]

This is a simple example before considering fees and taxes.
1.2 Why do people invest in Bitcoin?
People invest in Bitcoin for different reasons:
1. Potential price appreciation: Investors may expect Bitcoin's price to increase over time.
2. Digital asset ownership: Bitcoin is a digital asset that can be held in a wallet.
3. Trading opportunities: Traders attempt to profit from price movements.
4. Portfolio diversification: Some investors include Bitcoin alongside other assets.
5. Global market access: Bitcoin is traded on online exchanges in many countries.
Remember that Bitcoin's price can also decrease. Investment does not guarantee profit.
1.3 Investment vs Trading
These two terms are related but different.
Basis	Bitcoin investment	Bitcoin trading
Meaning	Purchasing Bitcoin as an investment	Buying and selling to benefit from price movements
Time period	Often longer term	Often shorter term
Main objective	Potential long-term appreciation	Profit from price changes
Activity	May involve holding Bitcoin	May involve frequent buying and selling
Example	Buy Bitcoin and hold for two years	Buy Bitcoin today and sell when its price changes

Easy way to remember:
- Investment = Buy and hold.
- Trading = Buy and sell based on price movements.

2.4 How does a Bitcoin exchange work?
Let's understand with an example.

Suppose Aman wants to purchase Bitcoin worth ₹5,000.

Step 1: Create an account

Aman creates an account on a cryptocurrency exchange.

Step 2: Complete required verification

The exchange may require identity verification according to its rules and applicable regulations.

Aman deposits money using a supported payment method.

He selects the Bitcoin trading pair, such as BTC/INR.

He enters the amount of Bitcoin he wants to purchase and chooses an order type.

2.5 Types of Bitcoin exchanges
For basic understanding, exchanges can be classified into two broad categories.
1. Centralised exchanges (CEX)
A centralised exchange is operated by a company or organisation that manages the trading platform.

Examples include:
- Coinbase
- Kraken
- Binance
The exchange generally manages user accounts, order matching and other platform operations.
2. Decentralised exchanges (DEX)
A decentralised exchange allows users to trade cryptocurrencies through blockchain-based protocols, often using smart contracts.

Users generally interact through their own wallets rather than relying on a central company to hold and manage every aspect of their funds.
Difference between CEX and DEX

Topic 3: Buying and Selling Bitcoin
3.1 How to buy Bitcoin?
Buying Bitcoin means exchanging traditional money or another cryptocurrency for Bitcoin.

For example:

Aman wants to purchase Bitcoin worth ₹10,000.

Suppose the market price is:
\[
1\ BTC=₹50,00,000
\]

The amount of Bitcoin he receives before fees is:
\[
BTC=\frac{10,000}{50,00,000}
\]

\[
\boxed{BTC=0.002}
\]

Therefore, Aman receives 0.002 BTC before applicable fees.
3.2 How to sell Bitcoin?
Selling Bitcoin means exchanging the Bitcoin you own for traditional money or another cryptocurrency.

Suppose Aman owns:
\[
0.002\ BTC
\]

The selling price is:
\[
1\ BTC=₹55,00,000
\]

The selling value is:
\[
0.002\times55,00,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₹11,000}
\]

His original purchase cost was ₹10,000.

Therefore:
\[
Profit=11,000-10,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₹1,000}
\]

This calculation ignores fees, taxes and any difference between the quoted price and actual execution price.
3.3 Important terms
Term	Meaning
Buying price	Price at which Bitcoin is purchased
Selling price	Price at which Bitcoin is sold
Profit	Selling value minus total cost
Loss	Total cost minus selling value
Exchange fee	Fee charged for a transaction
Trading pair	Two currencies being traded, such as BTC/INR
Liquidity	How easily an asset can be bought or sold without greatly affecting its price

First, understand what a position means.

A position is the trading exposure a trader takes in the market.

There are two important positions:
1. Long position.
2. Short position.
4.1 What is a long position?
A long position is a trading position in which a trader expects the price of Bitcoin to increase.

In simple words:

Buy first, expecting the price to rise.
Example
Aman purchases Bitcoin at ₹50,00,000 per BTC.

He expects its price to increase.

After some time, the price reaches ₹55,00,000.

He sells his Bitcoin.

His profit per BTC is:
\[
55,00,000-50,00,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₹5,00,000}
\]

Long position
Buying price₹50 L

Selling price₹55 L


Price increases → Profit

4.2 What is a short position?
A short position is a trading position in which a trader expects the price of Bitcoin to decrease.

In simple words:

The trader takes a position that benefits if the price falls.

In a conventional short sale, the trader borrows an asset, sells it and later buys it back to return it.
Example
Suppose Aman takes a short position when Bitcoin is priced at ₹50,00,000.

The price falls to ₹45,00,000.

He buys Bitcoin back at the lower price to close his position.

His profit before costs is:
\[
50,00,000-45,00,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₹5,00,000}
\]

Short position
Opening price₹50 L

Closing price₹45 L


Price decreases → Profit

If the price increases instead, a short position can result in a loss.
4.3 Difference between long and short positions
Basis	Long position	Short position
Expectation	Price will increase	Price will decrease
Basic action	Buy and later sell	Sell/short and later buy back
Profit when	Price rises	Price falls
Loss when	Price falls	Price rises
Market view	Bullish	Bearish

Memory trick:
- Long = Expect price to go up.
- Short = Expect price to go down.

Topic 5: Other Bitcoin Trading Choices
Your textbook also mentions CFDs, spread betting and margin trading. These are additional trading methods offered by some platforms. 
Mastering_Blockchain_Third_Edition.pdf

Let's understand each one.
5.1 CFDs — Contracts for Difference
CFD stands for Contract for Difference.

A CFD is a financial contract between a trader and a provider in which the parties settle the difference between the opening and closing prices of an asset.

In simple words:

You do not necessarily purchase or own actual Bitcoin. Instead, you enter into a contract based on Bitcoin's price movement.
Example
Suppose Aman enters a Bitcoin CFD when the price is ₹50,00,000.

He closes the contract when the price becomes ₹52,00,000.

The price difference is:
\[
52,00,000-50,00,000=₹2,00,000
\]

The contract's profit or loss depends on the position size and contract terms.

If Aman had taken a short position, the price increase would create a loss instead.
Important features
1. Does not necessarily involve owning actual Bitcoin.
2. Allows traders to speculate on price movements.
3. Can support both long and short positions.
4. May involve leverage.
5. Can create substantial losses.
5.2 Spread betting
Spread betting is a form of financial trading in which a person bets on whether the price of an asset will rise or fall, with profit or loss determined by the size of the price movement and the amount staked per unit of movement.
Example
Suppose Aman believes Bitcoin's price will rise.

He stakes ₹100 for every ₹1,000 increase in the quoted price.

If the price increases by ₹5,000:
\[
Profit=5\times₹100
\]

\[
\boxed{₹500}
\]

If the price falls by ₹5,000, the loss would be ₹500, before charges.

Important: Spread betting is not the same as purchasing and owning Bitcoin. Its availability and legal treatment vary by country.
5.3 Margin trading
Margin trading is a trading method in which a trader uses borrowed funds from an exchange or broker to take a larger position than their own available capital would normally allow.

Let's understand through a simple example.

Aman has ₹10,000.

The exchange allows him to trade with 5× leverage.

Therefore:
\[
Trading\ Position=10,000\times5
\]

\[
\boxed{₹50,000}
\]

He can control a ₹50,000 position using ₹10,000 of his own capital.
Why is margin trading risky?
Suppose the price moves against his position.

Even a relatively small price movement can create a large loss relative to his own capital.

The exchange may also liquidate his position if the available margin becomes insufficient.
Important terms
- Margin: Money provided by the trader as collateral.
- Leverage: The multiplier that increases exposure.
- Liquidation: Forced closing of a position when margin requirements are not maintained.
5.4 Comparison of trading methods

Short position	Benefit from a decrease in price	Not necessarily

Topic 6: Bitcoin Order Book
Very important 10-mark topic
This is one of the most important topics in the textbook's Bitcoin investment section.
6.1 What is an order book?
First understand this through an everyday example.

Suppose you want to sell your old mobile phone.

You say:

"I am willing to sell it for ₹10,000."

Another person says:

"I am willing to buy it for ₹9,000."

A third person says:

"I can pay ₹9,500."

All these buy and sell offers represent demand and supply.

A Bitcoin exchange maintains a similar list of buying and selling orders.

An order book is a list of all outstanding buy and sell orders for a particular trading pair on an exchange.

It shows the prices and quantities at which buyers want to purchase and sellers want to sell Bitcoin.

Your textbook illustrates an order book using the CEX.io exchange. 
Mastering_Blockchain_Third_Edition.pdf

6.2 Two main parts of an order book
An order book has two major sides:
1. Buy orders (Bids).
2. Sell orders (Asks).
A. Buy orders — Bids
A bid is an offer made by a buyer who wants to purchase Bitcoin at a particular price.

Example:

Aman wants to purchase 0.01 BTC at ₹50,00,000 per BTC.

He places a buy order.

That order appears on the buy side of the order book.
B. Sell orders — Asks
An ask is an offer made by a seller who wants to sell Bitcoin at a particular price.

Example:

Rahul wants to sell 0.01 BTC at ₹50,50,000 per BTC.

He places a sell order.

That order appears on the sell side.

6.4 How does an order book work?
Suppose:
- Buyer is willing to pay ₹50,00,000.
- Seller is willing to sell at ₹50,10,000.
The prices do not match.

Therefore, the trade will not execute immediately.

Now suppose the buyer increases the bid to ₹50,10,000.

The buyer's bid and seller's ask match.

A trade can occur.

This is called order matching.
6.5 What happens when bid and ask prices match?
When a suitable buy order and sell order match, the exchange executes the trade.

For example:
\[
Bid=₹50,10,000
\]

\[
Ask=₹50,10,000
\]

Since both prices are equal, the order can be matched, subject to available quantities and exchange rules.

Remember: An order book provides transparency about the buying and selling interest in a market.

Topic 7: Bid Price and Ask Price
Very important 5-mark question
7.1 What is a bid price?
Bid price is the highest price that a buyer is currently willing to pay for Bitcoin.

Example:

A buyer wants to purchase Bitcoin at ₹50,00,000.

His bid price is ₹50,00,000.
7.2 What is an ask price?
Ask price is the lowest price at which a seller is currently willing to sell Bitcoin.

Example:

A seller wants to sell Bitcoin at ₹50,10,000.

His ask price is ₹50,10,000.
7.3 What is bid-ask spread?
Bid-ask spread is the difference between the lowest ask price and the highest bid price.

Formula:
\[
Spread=Ask-Bid
\]

Example:
- Best bid = ₹50,00,000
- Best ask = ₹50,10,000
\[
Spread=50,10,000-50,00,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Spread=₹10,000}
\]

7.4 Why is the bid-ask spread important?
1. It indicates the difference between buying and selling prices.
2. It helps traders understand market liquidity.
3. A smaller spread generally indicates a more liquid market.
4. A larger spread can indicate lower liquidity or greater market uncertainty.
7.5 Difference between bid and ask
Basis	Bid	Ask
Meaning	Buyer's offered price	Seller's offered price
Represents	Demand	Supply
Placed by	Buyer	Seller
Typical relationship	Lower	Higher
Example	₹50,00,000	₹50,10,000

Memory trick:
- Bid = Buyer.
- Ask = Seller.

Topic 8: Types of Bitcoin Orders
Very important 10-mark question
Your textbook identifies two common order types:
1. Market orders.
2. Limit orders. 
   Mastering_Blockchain_Third_Edition.pdf
Let's understand both carefully.
8.1 What is a market order?
A market order is an order to buy or sell Bitcoin immediately at the best available prices in the order book.

In simple words:

You want to buy or sell immediately rather than wait for a specific price.
Example
Suppose Bitcoin's order book looks like this:
Seller's price	Available quantity
₹50,00,000	0.01 BTC
₹50,10,000	0.02 BTC
₹50,20,000	0.03 BTC

Aman places a market buy order for 0.01 BTC.

The exchange matches his order with the best available ask.

He purchases 0.01 BTC at ₹50,00,000, assuming the quantity is still available.
Important features
1. Designed for immediate execution.
2. Uses available orders in the order book.
3. Does not guarantee a specific execution price.
4. Can experience slippage if liquidity is insufficient.
8.2 What is a limit order?
A limit order is an order to buy or sell a specified quantity of Bitcoin at a specified price or better.

In simple words:

You decide the price at which you are willing to trade.
Example 1: Limit buy order
Suppose Bitcoin is currently trading at ₹50,00,000.

Aman does not want to purchase at that price.

He wants to purchase only when the price reaches ₹49,00,000 or lower.

He places a limit buy order at ₹49,00,000.

The order remains in the order book until:
- A suitable seller matches it, or
- It is cancelled or expires according to its order conditions.
Example 2: Limit sell order
Suppose Rahul owns Bitcoin and wants to sell it only at ₹55,00,000 or higher.

He places a limit sell order at ₹55,00,000.

The order will execute if a suitable buyer is available at that price or better.
Important features
1. Specifies a desired price.
2. Provides control over the execution price.
3. May not execute immediately.
4. Can remain open until executed or cancelled.
5. Can have a specified validity period.
8.3 Difference between market order and limit order
Basis	Market order	Limit order
Meaning	Buy or sell at the best available market price	Buy or sell at a specified price or better
Execution	Usually immediate if liquidity is available	Only when matching conditions are met
Price control	No guaranteed specific price	Specifies a price limit
Execution certainty	Generally higher	Not guaranteed
Waiting	Normally no waiting for a target price	May remain open
Example	Buy BTC immediately	Buy BTC only at ₹49 lakh or lower

8.4 What is order expiry?
Your textbook also mentions that a limit order can be left open for a specified period.

If it is not executed during that period, it can be cancelled.

For example:

Aman places a limit buy order at ₹49,00,000.

He specifies that the order should remain open for 24 hours.

If no suitable seller is available within 24 hours, the order is cancelled under that condition.

Topic 10: Advantages and Risks of Bitcoin Investment
Advantages
1. Potential returns: Bitcoin's price may increase, creating opportunities for profit.
2. Global trading: Bitcoin is available through exchanges in many countries.
3. Trading flexibility: Users can buy, sell and trade Bitcoin.
4. Market information: Exchanges provide charts, order books and market data.
5. Different trading methods: Some platforms offer spot trading, long and short positions and derivatives.
Risks and limitations
1. Price volatility: Bitcoin's price can rise or fall sharply.
2. Market risk: Investors may lose money due to unfavourable price movements.
3. Exchange risk: An exchange may face security, operational or financial problems.
4. Leverage risk: Margin trading can magnify both profits and losses.
5. Liquidity risk: It may be difficult to execute a large order at the expected price.
6. Regulatory risk: Rules governing cryptocurrency trading differ across countries.
7. Technical risk: Users can lose access to their Bitcoin through lost keys, phishing or other security problems.
Important: In this chapter, we are studying the working and concepts of Bitcoin investment, not recommending that you buy or trade Bitcoin.
