## 6.1 Divisors and factors

An integer \\(d\\) is a divisor of an integer \\(n\\), written \\(d\mid n\\), if there exists an integer \\(k\\) such that

\\[ n=dk. \\]

For example, the positive divisors of \\(12\\) are

\\[ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 6,\ 12. \\]

The positive divisors of \\(13\\) are

\\[ 1,\ 13. \\]

This difference leads to the definitions of prime and composite numbers.

## 6.2 Prime numbers

Definition: A prime number is a positive integer greater than \\(1\\) that has exactly two distinct positive divisors: \\(1\\) and itself.

Examples:

- \\(2\\): divisors \\(1,2\\), so it is prime.
- \\(3\\): divisors \\(1,3\\), so it is prime.
- \\(7\\): divisors \\(1,7\\), so it is prime.
- \\(13\\): divisors \\(1,13\\), so it is prime.

A prime number cannot be expressed as a product of two positive integers both greater than \\(1\\).

For example, \\(13\\) cannot be written as \\(a b\\) with both \\(a>1\\) and \\(b>1\\) integers.

### The special case of 2

\\[ \boxed{2\text{ is the only even prime number.}} \\]

Every even integer greater than \\(2\\) is divisible by \\(2\\) and has at least the three distinct positive divisors \\(1,2,n\\). Therefore, it is composite.

Every prime greater than \\(2\\) must be odd. However, not every odd number is prime: \\(9,15,21,25\\) are all odd composite numbers.

## 6.3 Composite numbers

Definition: A positive integer greater than \\(1\\) that is not prime is called composite.&#x20;

Equivalently, an integer \\(n>1\\) is composite if and only if there exists an integer \\(a\\) satisfying

\\[ a\mid n,\qquad 1\<a\<n. \\]

That is, it has a divisor other than \\(1\\) and itself.

Examples:

- \\(4=2\times2\\), so \\(4\\) is composite.
- \\(15=3\times5\\), so \\(15\\) is composite.
- \\(21=3\times7\\), so \\(21\\) is composite.
- \\(49=7\times7\\), so \\(49\\) is composite.

A number can have repeated prime factors and still be composite. For example, \\(49=7^2\\).

### Prime versus composite

| Property                                                    | Prime              | Composite          |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------ |
| Allowed values                                              | Integers \\(n>1\\) | Integers \\(n>1\\) |
| Positive divisors                                           | Exactly \\(1,n\\)  | More than two      |
| Example                                                     | \\(17\\)           | \\(18\\)           |
| Can be factored into smaller integers greater than \\(1\\)? | No                 | Yes                |

## 6.4 Why 1 is neither prime nor composite

This is a frequent MCQ trap.

The integer \\(1\\) has exactly one positive divisor: itself.

- It is not prime, because a prime must have exactly two distinct positive divisors.
- It is not composite, because the definition of composite requires an integer greater than \\(1\\).

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{1\text{ is neither prime nor composite.}} \\]

The number \\(0\\) is also neither prime nor composite under these definitions because it is not a positive integer greater than \\(1\\).

## 6.5 A useful property: prime factors of a composite number

Every composite positive integer can be expressed as a product of two integers greater than \\(1\\). Repeating this factorization eventually produces prime factors.

For example,

\\[ \begin{aligned} 84&=2\times42\\\ &=2\times2\times21\\\ &=2\times2\times3\times7. \end{aligned} \\]

Thus, the prime factors of \\(84\\) are \\(2,3,7\\), with \\(2\\) appearing twice.

The existence and uniqueness of such prime factorizations are formalized by the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, which is the next module's main topic.

## 6.6 A mathematical test for primality

For a positive integer \\(n>1\\), if \\(n\\) is composite, then it has a divisor \\(d\\) such that

\\[ 1\<d\leq\sqrt n. \\]

Why? If \\(n=ab\\) is composite, with \\(1\<a\leq b\<n\\), then \\(a^2\leq ab=n\\). Hence \\(a\leq\sqrt n\\).

This gives an important result:

\\[ \boxed{\text{To test whether }n\text{ is prime, it is enough to check possible divisors up to }\sqrt n.} \\]

For example, consider \\(29\\). Since

\\[ \sqrt{29}\approx5.39, \\]

we need to test prime divisors no larger than \\(5\\): \\(2,3,5\\).

- \\(29\\) is not divisible by \\(2\\).
- Its digit sum is \\(2+9=11\\), so it is not divisible by \\(3\\).
- It does not end in \\(0\\) or \\(5\\), so it is not divisible by \\(5\\).

Therefore, \\(29\\) is prime.

We will develop this method more fully in Module 8, which covers trial division and the Sieve of Eratosthenes.

## Module 6: Final revision sheet

- Prime: integer greater than \\(1\\) with exactly two distinct positive divisors.
- Composite: integer greater than \\(1\\) that is not prime.
- \\(1\\) is neither prime nor composite.
- \\(2\\) is the only even prime.
- An odd integer need not be prime.
- \\(n>1\\) is composite exactly when it has a divisor \\(d\\) with \\(1\<d\<n\\).
- To test primality, checking prime divisors up to \\(\sqrt n\\) is sufficient.
