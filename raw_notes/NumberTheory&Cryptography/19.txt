# **Unit IV — Module 2: Classical Cryptography and Substitution Ciphers**

Primary reference: Rosen, *Number Theory and Cryptography*, Section 4.6 — Classical Cryptography.

This module studies the basic idea behind classical ciphers, especially **substitution**: replacing plaintext letters with other letters according to a fixed rule.

We'll distinguish general substitution from shift, affine and Vigenère ciphers, which we'll study in detail in later modules.

## **2.1 What is classical cryptography?**

Classical cryptography refers to older methods of securing messages, developed long before modern computer-based cryptography.

Rosen explains that classical ciphers transform messages by changing individual letters or blocks of letters into other letters or blocks. These methods were used extensively until the 20th century.&#x20;

U456 (Textbook content) MTH381.pdf



Two fundamental methods are:

1. **Substitution:** replace letters or groups of letters.
2. **Transposition:** rearrange the positions of letters.

The distinction is important because the two methods transform different properties of the message.

## **2.2 Substitution cipher**

A substitution cipher replaces each plaintext symbol with another symbol.

For example, suppose the substitution rule is:

\\[ A\rightarrow Q,\quad B\rightarrow W,\quad C\rightarrow E \\]

Then the plaintext

\\[ \text{CAB} \\]

becomes

\\[ \text{EQW}. \\]

The original positions are preserved: the first plaintext letter becomes the first ciphertext letter, and so on.

### **General mathematical representation**

Let the plaintext alphabet be

\\[ \mathcal{A}=\\{A,B,C,\ldots,Z\\}. \\]

A substitution cipher defines a mapping

\\[ S:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}. \\]

For encryption to be reversible, every plaintext letter must map to a unique ciphertext letter, and every ciphertext letter must correspond to exactly one plaintext letter.

In mathematical language, \\(S\\) must be a **bijection** (one-to-one and onto).

Its inverse mapping \\(S^{-1}\\) performs decryption:

\\[ C=S(P) \\]

\\[ \boxed{P=S^{-1}(C)} \\]

## **2.3 Monoalphabetic substitution cipher**

A **monoalphabetic substitution cipher** uses one fixed substitution rule throughout the entire message.

The word *monoalphabetic* means that each plaintext letter is always replaced by the same ciphertext letter.

For example, suppose the substitution is:

| Plaintext  | A | B | C | D | E | F |
| ---------- | - | - | - | - | - | - |
| Ciphertext | Q | W | E | R | T | Y |

Then:

- A always becomes Q.
- B always becomes W.
- C always becomes E.
- The same mapping is used wherever that letter appears.

If plaintext contains `ABCA`, its ciphertext is `QWEQ`.

Notice that the first and fourth ciphertext letters are identical because the first and fourth plaintext letters are both A.

### **Mathematical representation**

Let the alphabet be represented by the set

\\[ \mathbb Z\_{26}=\\{0,1,2,\ldots,25\\}. \\]

A monoalphabetic substitution is a permutation of these 26 symbols.

For encryption, we use a bijection:

\\[ S:\mathbb Z\_{26}\rightarrow\mathbb Z\_{26}. \\]

For decryption, we use its inverse:

\\[ S^{-1}:\mathbb Z\_{26}\rightarrow\mathbb Z\_{26}. \\]

Thus,

\\[ \boxed{C=S(P)} \\]

and

\\[ \boxed{P=S^{-1}(C)}. \\]

The requirement that \\(S\\) be one-to-one is essential. If two different plaintext letters were mapped to the same ciphertext letter, decryption could become ambiguous.

### **How many possible substitution keys exist?**

There are 26 choices for the ciphertext symbol replacing A, 25 remaining choices for B, 24 for C, and so on.

Therefore, the number of possible monoalphabetic substitution keys is

\\[ \boxed{26!} \\]

\\[ 26!=403291461126605635584000000. \\]

This is approximately

\\[ 4.03\times10^{26}. \\]

This is a very large keyspace. However, a large keyspace alone does not guarantee security.

Why? Because monoalphabetic substitution preserves the frequency pattern of letters.

## **2.4 Why monoalphabetic substitution is vulnerable**

Suppose a plaintext contains the letter E many times.

Since a monoalphabetic substitution always replaces E with the same ciphertext letter, that ciphertext letter will also occur many times.

Therefore, even if the attacker does not know the substitution key, the statistical characteristics of the original language remain visible.

This is the basis of **frequency analysis**.

### **English letter frequency**

Rosen gives the following approximate frequencies for the nine most common English letters:

| E      | 13%                   |
| T      | 9%                    |
| A      | 8%                    |

| I      | 7%                    |
| N      | 7%                    |
| S      | 7%                    |
| H      | 6%                    |
| R      | 6%                    |

These are approximate frequencies, not fixed percentages for every text.&#x20;

### **Frequency analysis procedure**

Suppose an attacker intercepts a long ciphertext.

1. Count how many times each ciphertext letter appears.
2. Calculate or compare their relative frequencies.
3. Identify the most frequent ciphertext letters.
4. Hypothesize which common plaintext letters they represent.
5. Attempt decryption using the hypothesized mappings.
6. Check whether the resulting text is meaningful.
7. Test alternative hypotheses if necessary.

**Important:** Frequency analysis gives hypotheses, not guaranteed answers. A short message may not contain enough data for reliable frequency estimates.

## **2.5 Rosen's worked cryptanalysis example**

Rosen gives the following intercepted ciphertext, known to have been encrypted using a shift cipher:

INTERCEPTED CIPHERTEXT

# **ZNK KGXRE HOXJ MKZY ZNK CUXS**

We are told that the encryption method is a shift cipher.

**Step 1: Identify the most frequent ciphertext letter.**

The most frequent letter is K.

**Step 2: Make a hypothesis.**

Since E is the most common English letter, hypothesize that plaintext E was encrypted as ciphertext K.

Using the mapping \\(A=0,B=1,\ldots,Z=25\\):

\\[ E=4,\qquad K=10. \\]

**Step 3: Find the shift.**

The shift is

\\[ k\equiv10-4\pmod{26}. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{k=6}. \\]

**Step 4: Decrypt by shifting backward by six.**

The ciphertext becomes:

\\[ \boxed{\text{THE EARLY BIRD GETS THE WORM.}} \\]

The recovered message makes sense, supporting the hypothesis that the key is 6.&#x20;

Notice that this example demonstrates frequency analysis against a shift cipher. The same general idea can be used against monoalphabetic substitution, although a general substitution cipher requires identifying a larger set of letter mappings.

## **2.6 Character ciphers and block ciphers**

Rosen distinguishes between methods that replace individual characters and methods that replace blocks of characters.

### **A. Character / monoalphabetic ciphers**

These replace each individual letter with another letter.

Examples:

- Shift cipher
- Affine cipher

They are vulnerable to attacks based on letter-frequency analysis.

### **B. Block ciphers**

These replace blocks of letters with other blocks of letters rather than replacing each character individually.

For example, a block of four letters might be transformed as one unit.

Rosen introduces the transposition cipher as a simple block cipher.&#x20;

| Feature                   | Character cipher                                 | Block cipher                                               |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Input                     | Individual letters                               | Groups of letters                                          |
| Transformation            | One character at a time                          | One block at a time                                        |
| Example in Rosen          | Shift and affine                                 | Transposition                                              |
| Frequency characteristics | Individual letter frequencies may remain visible | Individual character patterns may be changed or rearranged |

Do not confuse the textbook's use of *block cipher* here with the much more advanced modern block-cipher designs. We'll study the specific permutation cipher in Module 7.

## **2.7 Substitution versus transposition — Important comparison**

| Property         | Substitution cipher  | Transposition cipher |
| ---------------- | -------------------- | -------------------- |
| Operation        | Replaces symbols     | Rearranges positions |
| Original letters | May change           | Remain unchanged     |
| Letter order     | Usually preserved    | Changed              |
| Key              | Substitution mapping | Permutation          |
| Example          | ABC → QWE            | ABC → CAB            |
| Decryption       | Inverse substitution | Inverse permutation  |

A useful way to remember:

- Substitution: **Which symbol?**
- Transposition: **Which position?**

## **2.8 Important examination points**

1. A monoalphabetic cipher uses one fixed substitution throughout the message.
2. A substitution key must define a bijection for unambiguous decryption.
3. The number of possible general monoalphabetic substitution keys is \\(26!\\).
4. A large keyspace does not automatically make a cipher secure.
5. Monoalphabetic substitution preserves individual letter frequencies.
6. Frequency analysis uses the statistical characteristics of language.
7. E is approximately 13% of English letters in Rosen's frequency table.
8. Cryptanalysis attempts to recover plaintext without the secret encryption information.
9. Shift and affine ciphers are character / monoalphabetic ciphers.
10. Transposition rearranges letters rather than replacing them.
