The Hill cipher is important because it introduces **matrix multiplication and matrix inverses modulo 26**. Unlike the previous ciphers, it encrypts groups of letters together.

## **6.1 What is the Hill cipher?**

The Hill cipher is a polygraphic substitution cipher developed by mathematician Lester S. Hill in 1929.

It encrypts a block of plaintext letters using a square key matrix.

For example, if we use a \\(2\times2\\) key matrix, we encrypt two letters at a time.

If we use a \\(3\times3\\) key matrix, we encrypt three letters at a time.

The main mathematical operations are:

1. Convert letters into numbers.
2. Arrange the numbers into column vectors.
3. Multiply the key matrix by the plaintext vector.
4. Reduce every resulting entry modulo 26.
5. Convert the resulting numbers back into letters.

The alphabet mapping is:

\\[ A=0,\ B=1,\ C=2,\ldots,Z=25. \\]

## **6.2 Mathematical representation**

Let the plaintext block be represented by the column vector

\\[ P= \begin{bmatrix} p_1\\\ p_2 \end{bmatrix} \\]

and let the encryption key be the \\(2\times2\\) matrix

\\[ K= \begin{bmatrix} a&b\\\ c&d \end{bmatrix}. \\]

The ciphertext vector is

\\[ \boxed{C=KP\pmod{26}} \\]

This is the fundamental Hill cipher encryption formula.

Expanded:

\\[ \begin{bmatrix} c_1\\\ c_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a&b\\\ c&d \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} p_1\\\ p_2 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26}. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{c_1=(ap_1+bp_2)\bmod26} \\]

\\[ \boxed{c_2=(cp_1+dp_2)\bmod26} \\]

Notice that each ciphertext letter depends on both plaintext letters. This is the main difference from the shift, affine and Vigenère ciphers.

## **6.3 First worked example — Encrypt a two-letter block**

Given the key matrix:

\\[ K= \begin{bmatrix} 3&3\\\ 2&5 \end{bmatrix} \\]

Encrypt the plaintext block `HI`.

**Step 1: Convert the plaintext into numbers.**

\\[ H=7,\qquad I=8. \\]

Thus,

\\[ P= \begin{bmatrix} 7\\\ 8 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

**Step 2: Write the encryption formula.**

\\[ C=KP\pmod{26}. \\]

Substitute:

\\[ C= \begin{bmatrix} 3&3\\\ 2&5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 7\\\ 8 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26}. \\]

**Step 3: Multiply the matrices.**

First row:

\\[ 3(7)+3(8)=21+24=45. \\]

Second row:

\\[ 2(7)+5(8)=14+40=54. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ C= \begin{bmatrix} 45\\\ 54 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26}. \\]

**Step 4: Reduce modulo 26.**

\\[ 45\bmod26=19 \\]

\\[ 54\bmod26=2. \\]

Hence,

\\[ C= \begin{bmatrix} 19\\\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

**Step 5: Convert numbers into letters.**

\\[ 19=T,\qquad 2=C. \\]

ENCRYPTION RESULT

# **HI → TC**

This is the complete mathematical procedure for encrypting a two-letter block.

## **6.4 Why must the key matrix be invertible?**

Encryption alone is not sufficient. The receiver must be able to recover the original plaintext.

From

\\[ C=KP\pmod{26}, \\]

we want to recover \\(P\\).

If the inverse matrix \\(K^{-1}\\) exists modulo 26, multiply both sides by it:

\\[ K^{-1}C=K^{-1}KP\pmod{26}. \\]

Since

\\[ K^{-1}K=I, \\]

where \\(I\\) is the identity matrix,

\\[ \boxed{P=K^{-1}C\pmod{26}}. \\]

Thus, the key matrix must have an inverse modulo 26.

### **Condition for a 2 × 2 key matrix**

For

\\[ K= \begin{bmatrix} a&b\\\ c&d \end{bmatrix}, \\]

its determinant is

\\[ \boxed{\det(K)=ad-bc}. \\]

The matrix has an inverse modulo 26 if and only if

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(\det(K),26)=1}. \\]

This is a very important MCQ condition.

Why?

The ordinary matrix inverse formula involves division by the determinant. In modular arithmetic, division by a number is possible only when that number has a multiplicative inverse modulo 26.

Therefore, the determinant must be relatively prime to 26.

Since

\\[ 26=2\times13, \\]

the determinant must be divisible by neither 2 nor 13.

**Examples:**

| Determinant | GCD with 26 | Matrix invertible? |
| ----------- | ----------- | ------------------ |
| 5           | 1           | Yes                |
| 7           | 1           | Yes                |
| 9           | 1           | Yes                |
| 10          | 2           | No                 |
| 13          | 13          | No                 |
| 15          | 1           | Yes                |
| 26          | 26          | No                 |

## **6.5 Finding the inverse of a 2 × 2 key matrix**

For a matrix

\\[ K= \begin{bmatrix} a&b\\\ c&d \end{bmatrix}, \\]

the inverse modulo 26 is

\\[ \boxed{ K^{-1}=(\det K)^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} d&-b\\\ -c&a \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26} } \\]

This formula has two important parts:

1. Find the determinant and its modular inverse.
2. Multiply that inverse by the adjugate matrix.

### **Worked example**

Find the inverse of

\\[ K= \begin{bmatrix} 3&3\\\ 2&5 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26}. \\]

**Step 1: Calculate the determinant.**

\\[ \det(K)=(3)(5)-(3)(2) \\]

\\[ =15-6=9. \\]

**Step 2: Check whether the inverse exists.**

\\[ \gcd(9,26)=1. \\]

Therefore, the inverse exists.

**Step 3: Find the inverse of the determinant modulo 26.**

We need:

\\[ 9x\equiv1\pmod{26}. \\]

Since

\\[ 9\times3=27\equiv1\pmod{26}, \\]

we get

\\[ \boxed{9^{-1}=3\pmod{26}}. \\]

**Step 4: Form the adjugate matrix.**

For a 2 × 2 matrix, interchange the diagonal elements and change the signs of the off-diagonal elements:

\\[ \operatorname{adj}(K)= \begin{bmatrix} 5&-3\\\ -2&3 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

**Step 5: Multiply by the inverse of the determinant.**

\\[ K^{-1} = 3 \begin{bmatrix} 5&-3\\\ -2&3 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26}. \\]

\\[ = \begin{bmatrix} 15&-9\\\ -6&9 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26}. \\]

Convert negative entries to their nonnegative residues:

\\[ -9\bmod26=17 \\]

\\[ -6\bmod26=20. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{ K^{-1}= \begin{bmatrix} 15&17\\\ 20&9 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26} } \\]

**Verification:** A valid inverse must satisfy

\\[ KK^{-1}\equiv I\pmod{26}. \\]

Indeed,

\\[ \begin{bmatrix} 3&3\\\ 2&5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 15&17\\\ 20&9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 105&78\\\ 130&79 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

Taking each entry modulo 26:

\\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

So the inverse is correct.

## **6.6 Complete decryption example**

We previously encrypted:

\\[ \text{HI}\rightarrow\text{TC} \\]

using

\\[ K= \begin{bmatrix} 3&3\\\ 2&5 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

Now decrypt `TC`.

**Step 1: Convert ciphertext into a vector.**

\\[ T=19,\qquad C=2. \\]

\\[ C= \begin{bmatrix} 19\\\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

**Step 2: Use the inverse matrix.**

\\[ K^{-1}= \begin{bmatrix} 15&17\\\ 20&9 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

**Step 3: Apply the decryption formula.**

\\[ P=K^{-1}C\pmod{26}. \\]

\\[ P= \begin{bmatrix} 15&17\\\ 20&9 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 19\\\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26}. \\]

Multiply:

\\[ P= \begin{bmatrix} 15(19)+17(2)\\\ 20(19)+9(2) \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26}. \\]

\\[ = \begin{bmatrix} 319\\\ 398 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26}. \\]

Reduce:

\\[ 319\bmod26=7 \\]

\\[ 398\bmod26=8. \\]

Thus,

\\[ P= \begin{bmatrix} 7\\\ 8 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

Convert back to letters:

\\[ 7=H,\qquad8=I. \\]

DECRYPTION RESULT

# **TC → HI**

The original plaintext is successfully recovered.

## **6.7 Hill cipher with a 3 × 3 key matrix**

The Hill cipher is not limited to two-letter blocks.

For a three-letter block, use a \\(3\times3\\) key matrix:

\\[ K= \begin{bmatrix} a&b&c\\\ d&e&f\\\ g&h&i \end{bmatrix} \\]

The plaintext is represented as:

\\[ P= \begin{bmatrix} p_1\\\ p_2\\\ p_3 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

Encryption is still:

\\[ \boxed{C=KP\pmod{26}}. \\]

Expanded:

\\[ \begin{aligned} c_1&=(ap_1+bp_2+cp_3)\bmod26\\\ c_2&=(dp_1+ep_2+fp_3)\bmod26\\\ c_3&=(gp_1+hp_2+ip_3)\bmod26. \end{aligned} \\]

The determinant of a 3 × 3 matrix is

\\[ \begin{aligned} \det(K)={}&aei+bfg+cdh\\\ &-ceg-bdi-afh. \end{aligned} \\]

The key matrix is invertible modulo 26 precisely when

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(\det(K),26)=1}. \\]

The same condition applies to square key matrices of any size.

**Important distinction:** A matrix may have a nonzero determinant and still fail to have an inverse modulo 26. For example, determinant 2 is nonzero, but \\(\gcd(2,26)=2\\), so it has no modular inverse.

## **6.8 Encrypting a complete message**

Suppose the key matrix is

\\[ K= \begin{bmatrix} 3&3\\\ 2&5 \end{bmatrix} \\]

and the plaintext is:

\\[ \text{HELP}. \\]

Since the key is \\(2\times2\\), divide the message into blocks of two letters:

\\[ \boxed{\text{HE}\quad\text{LP}} \\]

### **First block: HE**

\\[ H=7,\quad E=4. \\]

\\[ P_1= \begin{bmatrix} 7\\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

\\[ C_1= \begin{bmatrix} 3&3\\\ 2&5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 7\\\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26}. \\]

\\[ = \begin{bmatrix} 33\\\ 34 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26} = \begin{bmatrix} 7\\\ 8 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

This gives:

\\[ \text{HE}\rightarrow\text{HI}. \\]

### **Second block: LP**

\\[ L=11,\quad P=15. \\]

\\[ P_2= \begin{bmatrix} 11\\\ 15 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

\\[ C_2= \begin{bmatrix} 3&3\\\ 2&5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 11\\\ 15 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26}. \\]

\\[ = \begin{bmatrix} 78\\\ 97 \end{bmatrix} \pmod{26} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\\ 19 \end{bmatrix}. \\]

This gives:

\\[ \text{LP}\rightarrow\text{AT}. \\]

Therefore:

COMPLETE ENCRYPTION

# **HELP → HIAT**

The message is encrypted block by block, using the same key matrix for each block.

**What if the number of letters is not divisible by the block size?**

The final block must be completed to the required size. A padding letter or agreed padding convention is used. For example, with a block size of 2, `CAT` can be padded as `CATX`. The receiver must know the padding convention to remove the extra letter correctly.

## **6.9 Security and cryptanalysis of the Hill cipher**

The Hill cipher has an important advantage over simple monoalphabetic substitution ciphers: it encrypts groups of letters rather than individual letters.

This mixes the numerical values of multiple plaintext letters into the ciphertext.

However, the Hill cipher is vulnerable to a **known-plaintext attack**.

### **Known-plaintext attack**

Suppose an attacker knows some plaintext blocks and their corresponding ciphertext blocks.

For a two-dimensional Hill cipher:

\\[ C=KP\pmod{26}. \\]

If the attacker has two linearly independent plaintext vectors, they can form a plaintext matrix \\(P\\) and a corresponding ciphertext matrix \\(C\\):

\\[ C=KP\pmod{26}. \\]

If \\(P\\) is invertible modulo 26, then:

\\[ \boxed{K=CP^{-1}\pmod{26}}. \\]

This allows the attacker to recover the key matrix.

**Important:** The plaintext matrix must itself be invertible modulo 26. Merely having two known plaintext blocks is not sufficient.

### **Limitations of the Hill cipher**

- The key matrix must be invertible modulo 26.
- It is vulnerable to known-plaintext attacks.
- It is a classical cipher and is not considered secure for modern communications.
- It requires matrix arithmetic modulo 26.

## **6.10 Comparison of all classical ciphers covered so far**

| Cipher   | Mathematical operation                | Encryption unit          |
| -------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Shift    | Addition modulo 26                    | One letter               |
| Affine   | Multiplication and addition modulo 26 | One letter               |
| Vigenère | Position-dependent addition modulo 26 | Repeated-key positions   |
| Hill     | Matrix multiplication modulo 26       | Fixed-size letter blocks |

**Remember these distinctions:**

- Shift cipher: one fixed shift.
- Affine cipher: one fixed multiplication and addition.
- Vigenère cipher: different shifts according to a repeating keyword.
- Hill cipher: matrix multiplication combines multiple plaintext letters.
