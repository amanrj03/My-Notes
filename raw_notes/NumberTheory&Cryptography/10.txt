# Module 10: GCD, LCM and the Euclidean Algorithm

Number Theory-II · Rosen, Number Theory and Cryptography, Section 4.3

This module covers three closely related ideas:

1. Greatest common divisor (GCD): the largest positive integer dividing two integers.
2. Least common multiple (LCM): the smallest positive integer divisible by two positive integers.
3. Euclidean algorithm: an efficient method for computing the GCD without finding complete prime factorizations first.

We'll study definitions, theorems, proofs, worked examples, special cases, and MCQ traps using your textbook as the primary reference.&#x20;

## 10.1 Greatest common divisor (GCD)

### Definition

Let \\(a\\) and \\(b\\) be integers, not both zero. Their greatest common divisor, written

\\[ \gcd(a,b), \\]

is the largest positive integer that divides both \\(a\\) and \\(b\\).

For example, the positive divisors common to \\(18\\) and \\(30\\) are

\\[ 1,2,3,6. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(18,30)=6}. \\]

### Formal definition using divisibility

A positive integer \\(d\\) is \\(\gcd(a,b)\\) when:

1. \\(d\mid a\\) and \\(d\mid b\\).
2. Every common divisor of \\(a\\) and \\(b\\) divides \\(d\\).

The second condition is stronger than simply saying \\(d\\) is a common divisor. It ensures that \\(d\\) is the greatest common divisor.

### Common divisor versus greatest common divisor

For \\(18\\) and \\(30\\):

- \\(2\\) is a common divisor.
- \\(3\\) is a common divisor.
- \\(6\\) is a common divisor.
- \\(9\\) is not a common divisor because \\(9\nmid30\\).

Only \\(6\\) is the greatest common divisor.

MCQ trap: The GCD is not necessarily one of the numbers' prime factors considered separately; it is the largest positive integer dividing both numbers.

## 10.2 Relatively prime integers

Definition: Two integers \\(a\\) and \\(b\\) are relatively prime if

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(a,b)=1}. \\]

For example,

\\[ \gcd(17,22)=1, \\]

so \\(17\\) and \\(22\\) are relatively prime. Neither number needs to be prime itself.&#x20;

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf



### Important consequences

1\. Two distinct primes are relatively prime.

If \\(p\\) and \\(q\\) are distinct primes, their only common positive divisor is \\(1\\). Hence,

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(p,q)=1\quad(p\ne q)}. \\]

2\. Consecutive positive integers are relatively prime.

For every positive integer \\(n\\),

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(n,n+1)=1}. \\]

Proof: Any common divisor of \\(n\\) and \\(n+1\\) must divide their difference:

\\[ (n+1)-n=1. \\]

The only positive divisor of \\(1\\) is \\(1\\). Therefore, the GCD is \\(1\\).

3\. Relatively prime does not mean both numbers are prime.

For example,

\\[ \gcd(8,15)=1. \\]

Although both numbers are composite, they are relatively prime.

### Pairwise relatively prime

A set of integers \\(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_k\\) is pairwise relatively prime if every pair has GCD \\(1\\):

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(a_i,a_j)=1\quad\text{for every }i\<j}. \\]

For example, \\(10,17,21\\) are pairwise relatively prime because

\\[ \gcd(10,17)=1,\quad \gcd(10,21)=1,\quad \gcd(17,21)=1. \\]

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf

Subtle MCQ trap: Having a GCD of \\(1\\) for the entire set is not the definition of pairwise relative primality. You must check every pair.

For instance, the numbers \\(6,10,15\\) have no common divisor greater than \\(1\\) shared by all three, but they are not pairwise relatively prime:

\\[ \gcd(6,10)=2,\quad \gcd(6,15)=3,\quad \gcd(10,15)=5. \\]

## 10.3 GCD using prime factorization

Suppose two positive integers have prime factorizations

\\[ a=p_1^{\alpha_1}p_2^{\alpha_2}\cdots p_k^{\alpha_k} \\]

and

\\[ b=p_1^{\beta_1}p_2^{\beta_2}\cdots p_k^{\beta_k}. \\]

Include every prime appearing in either factorization, using exponent \\(0\\) when a prime is absent.

Then

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(a,b)= p_1^{\min(\alpha_1,\beta_1)} \cdots p_k^{\min(\alpha_k,\beta_k)}}. \\]

In words: take the smaller exponent of every prime factor.&#x20;

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf



### Why do we take minimum exponents?

A common divisor cannot contain a prime to a greater power than that prime occurs in either number. The greatest common divisor uses the largest power that fits into both factorizations, which is the smaller of the two exponents.

### Worked example

Find \\(\gcd(120,500)\\).

Prime factorizations:

\\[ 120=2^3\cdot3^1\cdot5^1 \\]

\\[ 500=2^2\cdot3^0\cdot5^3. \\]

Take minimum exponents:

\\[ \begin{aligned} \gcd(120,500) &=2^{\min(3,2)}   3^{\min(1,0)}   5^{\min(1,3)}\\\ &=2^2\cdot3^0\cdot5^1\\\ &=4\cdot1\cdot5. \end{aligned} \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(120,500)=20}. \\]

## 10.4 Least common multiple (LCM)

### Definition

For positive integers \\(a\\) and \\(b\\), the least common multiple, written \\(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)\\), is the smallest positive integer divisible by both \\(a\\) and \\(b\\).&#x20;

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf



For example, the positive multiples of \\(6\\) are

\\[ 6,12,18,24,30,36,\ldots \\]

and those of \\(8\\) are

\\[ 8,16,24,32,40,\ldots \\]

The smallest positive common multiple is \\(24\\), so

\\[ \boxed{\operatorname{lcm}(6,8)=24}. \\]

### LCM using prime factorization

Using the same factorizations as before,

\\[ a=p_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots p_k^{\alpha_k}, \qquad b=p_1^{\beta_1}\cdots p_k^{\beta_k}, \\]

the formula is

\\[ \boxed{\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)= p_1^{\max(\alpha_1,\beta_1)} \cdots p_k^{\max(\alpha_k,\beta_k)}}. \\]

In words: take the larger exponent of every prime factor.&#x20;

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf



Why? A common multiple must contain enough copies of every prime to be divisible by both numbers. The maximum exponent is the smallest exponent that satisfies both requirements.

### Worked example

Find \\(\operatorname{lcm}(120,500)\\).

We already have

\\[ 120=2^3\cdot3\cdot5, \qquad 500=2^2\cdot5^3. \\]

Take maximum exponents:

\\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{lcm}(120,500) &=2^{\max(3,2)}   3^{\max(1,0)}   5^{\max(1,3)}\\\ &=2^3\cdot3\cdot5^3\\\ &=8\cdot3\cdot125. \end{aligned} \\]

Thus,

\\[ \boxed{\operatorname{lcm}(120,500)=3000}. \\]

### GCD versus LCM: the exponent rule

| Quantity | Prime-factor exponent |
| -------- | --------------------- |
| GCD      | Minimum               |
| LCM      | Maximum               |

This is one of the most frequently tested distinctions in this topic.

## 10.5 The relationship between GCD and LCM

For any two positive integers \\(a\\) and \\(b\\), Rosen's Theorem 5 states

\\[ \boxed{ab=\gcd(a,b)\cdot\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)}. \\]

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf

Equivalently,

\\[ \boxed{\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)= \frac{ab}{\gcd(a,b)}}. \\]

And

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(a,b)= \frac{ab}{\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)}}. \\]

### Why is this identity true?

Consider the prime factorizations

\\[ a=\prod_i p_i^{\alpha_i}, \qquad b=\prod_i p_i^{\beta_i}. \\]

The product \\(ab\\) contains each prime \\(p_i\\) with exponent

\\[ \alpha_i+\beta_i. \\]

Meanwhile, the product of the GCD and LCM contains it with exponent

\\[ \min(\alpha_i,\beta_i)+ \max(\alpha_i,\beta_i). \\]

For any two numbers \\(\alpha_i,\beta_i\\),

\\[ \min(\alpha_i,\beta_i)+ \max(\alpha_i,\beta_i) =\alpha_i+\beta_i. \\]

So the two products have identical prime factorizations. By uniqueness of prime factorization, they are equal.

### Worked example

Suppose

\\[ a=84,\qquad b=126. \\]

Their prime factorizations are

\\[ 84=2^2\cdot3\cdot7, \qquad 126=2\cdot3^2\cdot7. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \gcd(84,126)=2\cdot3\cdot7=42 \\]

and

\\[ \operatorname{lcm}(84,126)=2^2\cdot3^2\cdot7=252. \\]

Verify the identity:

\\[ 84\cdot126=10584 \\]

and

\\[ 42\cdot252=10584. \\]

Hence the identity holds.

MCQ shortcut: If \\(a\\), \\(b\\), and their GCD are given, calculate the LCM using \\(ab/\gcd(a,b)\\). This is often faster than factorizing both numbers.

### Special case: relatively prime integers

If \\(\gcd(a,b)=1\\), then

\\[ \boxed{\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab}. \\]

For example, since \\(\gcd(8,15)=1\\),

\\[ \operatorname{lcm}(8,15)=8\cdot15=120. \\]

Be careful: \\(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab\\) is not true for every pair. It holds when the two positive integers are relatively prime.

## 10.6 The Euclidean algorithm

Prime factorization is mathematically useful, but finding complete factorizations can be difficult for large numbers. The Euclidean algorithm computes the GCD by repeatedly applying the division algorithm.&#x20;

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf



Its central theorem is:

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r)} \\]

whenever

\\[ a=bq+r. \\]

Here \\(q\\) is the quotient and \\(r\\) is the remainder.

### Lemma: Why does the GCD remain unchanged?

Suppose

\\[ a=bq+r. \\]

We will prove that \\(a,b\\) and \\(b,r\\) have exactly the same common divisors.

Direction 1: Suppose \\(d\mid a\\) and \\(d\mid b\\).

Since \\(d\\) divides \\(a\\) and \\(bq\\), it also divides their difference:

\\[ a-bq=r. \\]

Thus \\(d\mid r\\). Therefore, every common divisor of \\(a,b\\) is also a common divisor of \\(b,r\\).

Direction 2: Suppose \\(d\mid b\\) and \\(d\mid r\\).

Since \\(d\\) divides \\(bq\\) and \\(r\\), it divides their sum:

\\[ bq+r=a. \\]

Thus \\(d\mid a\\). Therefore, every common divisor of \\(b,r\\) is also a common divisor of \\(a,b\\).

Both pairs have the same common divisors, so they must have the same greatest common divisor:

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r)}. \\]

This is the key mathematical justification for the Euclidean algorithm.&#x20;

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf



### General procedure

To calculate \\(\gcd(a,b)\\), where \\(a\ge b>0\\):

1. Divide \\(a\\) by \\(b\\), writing \\(a=bq+r\\).
2. Replace the pair \\((a,b)\\) with \\((b,r)\\).
3. Repeat the division with the new pair.
4. Stop when the remainder is \\(0\\).
5. The last nonzero remainder is the GCD.

Why does the method terminate? Every nonzero remainder is a nonnegative integer strictly smaller than the divisor used to obtain it. The positive remainders therefore keep decreasing, so eventually a remainder of \\(0\\) must occur.

## 10.7 Worked example: \\(\gcd(287,91)\\)

This is the example used in your textbook.&#x20;

Start with the larger number divided by the smaller.

Step 1

\\[ 287=91(3)+14 \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \gcd(287,91)=\gcd(91,14). \\]

Step 2

\\[ 91=14(6)+7 \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \gcd(91,14)=\gcd(14,7). \\]

Step 3

\\[ 14=7(2)+0 \\]

The remainder is now \\(0\\). The last nonzero remainder is \\(7\\).

Hence,

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(287,91)=7}. \\]

## 10.8 A more involved example: \\(\gcd(12345,54321)\\)

Apply successive divisions:

\\[ 54321=12345(4)+4941 \\]

\\[ 12345=4941(2)+2463 \\]

\\[ 4941=2463(2)+15 \\]

\\[ 2463=15(164)+3 \\]

\\[ 15=3(5)+0. \\]

The last nonzero remainder is \\(3\\). Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(12345,54321)=3}. \\]

Notice how the numbers become smaller without requiring their prime factorizations. This is why the Euclidean algorithm is useful for large integers.

### What if the first division has remainder zero?

If \\(a=bq\\), then \\(b\mid a\\). In that case,

\\[ \gcd(a,b)=b \\]

for positive \\(a\ge b\\). The algorithm stops immediately, and the divisor \\(b\\) is the GCD.

For example,

\\[ 72=12(6)+0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \gcd(72,12)=12. \\]

## 10.9 GCD, LCM and the Euclidean algorithm: when to use each method

| Question type                                       | Most direct method                         |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Small numbers with easy factors                     | List common divisors                       |
| Numbers with known prime factorizations             | Minimum-exponent rule                      |
| Large integers whose factors are unknown            | Euclidean algorithm                        |
| Find LCM when GCD is known                          | \\(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab/\gcd(a,b)\\) |
| Check whether two numbers are relatively prime      | Calculate whether their GCD is \\(1\\)     |
| Prove two consecutive integers are relatively prime | Use their difference, which is \\(1\\)     |

## 10.10 Important edge cases and advanced MCQ traps

### A. GCD with zero

For any nonzero integer \\(a\\),

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(a,0)=|a|}. \\]

Why? Every divisor of \\(a\\) divides \\(0\\), because \\(0=a\cdot0\\). The largest positive common divisor is therefore \\(|a|\\).

Examples:

\\[ \gcd(17,0)=17,\qquad \gcd(-24,0)=24. \\]

The expression \\(\gcd(0,0)\\) is conventionally left undefined in this setting because there is no largest positive common divisor.

### B. GCD with negative integers

The GCD is always taken to be nonnegative, and for inputs not both zero, it is positive:

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(|a|,|b|)}. \\]

For example,

\\[ \gcd(-18,30)=\gcd(18,30)=6. \\]

### C. The remainder is not necessarily the GCD

In

\\[ a=bq+r, \\]

the remainder \\(r\\) preserves the GCD through \\(\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r)\\). But \\(r\\) itself need not equal the GCD.

For example,

\\[ 287=91(3)+14. \\]

The remainder is \\(14\\), but the GCD is \\(7\\).

### D. Pairwise relative primality versus a common GCD of \\(1\\)

For a set containing three or more numbers, these are different properties.

- Pairwise relatively prime: Every pair has GCD \\(1\\).
- Collectively having GCD \\(1\\): The GCD of the entire set is \\(1\\).

For example, \\(6,10,15\\) have collective GCD \\(1\\), but they are not pairwise relatively prime.

### E. LCM and GCD are not always equal to the smaller and larger inputs

It is not generally true that the GCD is the smaller input or that the LCM is the larger input. Those statements hold only in special divisibility cases.

For instance,

\\[ \gcd(12,18)=6,\qquad \operatorname{lcm}(12,18)=36. \\]

The GCD is smaller than both inputs, while the LCM is larger than both.
