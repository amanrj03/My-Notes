# Module 9: Prime Number Theorem and Prime Distribution

Number Theory-II · Based on Rosen, Number Theory and Cryptography, Section 4.3

This module is about how prime numbers are distributed among the positive integers. We'll cover the definitions, mathematical results, approximations, interpretations, limitations, and MCQ traps—not just memorize one formula.

The central question is:

As integers become larger, how many of them are prime?

The answer is not that primes stop appearing. There are infinitely many primes, but their proportion among the integers decreases.

## 9.1 Prime-counting function

The prime-counting function, written \\(\pi(x)\\), is defined as the number of primes less than or equal to the real number \\(x\\).

\\[ \boxed{\pi(x)=\\#\\{p:p\text{ is prime and }p\le x\\}} \\]

Here, \\(\\#\\) means “the number of elements in the set.”

### Examples

For \\(x=10\\), the primes not exceeding \\(10\\) are

\\[ 2,3,5,7. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{\pi(10)=4}. \\]

Similarly, the primes not exceeding \\(20\\) are

\\[ 2,3,5,7,11,13,17,19, \\]

so

\\[ \boxed{\pi(20)=8}. \\]

And, from our previous module,

\\[ \boxed{\pi(30)=10,\qquad \pi(100)=25}. \\]

### Important properties

Property 1: \\(\pi(x)\\) is nondecreasing.

If \\(x\le y\\), then

\\[ \boxed{\pi(x)\le\pi(y)}. \\]

Why? Increasing the upper limit cannot remove primes that were already counted.

Property 2: \\(\pi(x)\\) is a step function.

It increases by exactly one whenever \\(x\\) passes a prime number. It remains unchanged between consecutive primes.

For example,

\\[ \pi(10)=4,\quad \pi(11)=5,\quad \pi(12)=5. \\]

Property 3: The function counts primes, not all integers.

For example, \\(\pi(10)=4\\), not \\(10\\), because only four primes are at most \\(10\\).

## 9.2 The Prime Number Theorem (PNT)

The Prime Number Theorem describes the long-term distribution of prime numbers.

Statement from your textbook: The ratio of the number of primes not exceeding \\(x\\) to \\(x/\ln x\\) approaches \\(1\\) as \\(x\\) grows without bound. Here, \\(\ln x\\) denotes the natural logarithm.&#x20;

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf



Mathematically,

\\[ \boxed{\lim\_{x\to\infty} \frac{\pi(x)}{x/\ln x}=1} \\]

Equivalently,

\\[ \boxed{\pi(x)\sim\frac{x}{\ln x}} \\]

The symbol \\(\sim\\) means that the ratio of the two quantities approaches \\(1\\) as \\(x\to\infty\\). It does not mean that the quantities are exactly equal.

### What does the theorem tell us?

For sufficiently large \\(x\\), the number of primes not exceeding \\(x\\) can be approximated by

\\[ \boxed{\pi(x)\approx\frac{x}{\ln x}}. \\]

This is an approximation, not a formula for obtaining the exact number of primes.

### Example 1: Estimate the number of primes not exceeding 1,000

Using the Prime Number Theorem,

\\[ \pi(1000)\approx\frac{1000}{\ln1000}. \\]

Since

\\[ \ln1000\approx6.9078, \\]

we obtain

\\[ \pi(1000)\approx\frac{1000}{6.9078} \approx144.76. \\]

So the estimate is approximately \\(145\\) primes. The actual count is \\(168\\).

The difference is not a contradiction: the theorem gives an asymptotic approximation, not an exact count at every finite value.

### Example 2: Estimate the number of primes not exceeding 1,000,000

\\[ \pi(10^6)\approx\frac{10^6}{\ln(10^6)}. \\]

Using \\(\ln(10^6)=6\ln10\approx13.8155\\),

\\[ \pi(10^6)\approx \frac{1,000,000}{13.8155} \approx72,382. \\]

The actual count is \\(78,498\\). Again, the PNT estimate is useful for understanding the scale of the count, even though it is not exact.

MCQ distinction: If a question asks for an estimate using the PNT, use \\(x/\ln x\\). If it asks for the exact value of \\(\pi(x)\\), the approximation alone does not determine that value.

## 9.3 Why is the natural logarithm used?

The PNT specifically uses \\(\ln x\\), the logarithm to base \\(e\\), where \\(e\approx2.71828\\).

\\[ \boxed{\ln x=\log_e x} \\]

Recall the change-of-base formula:

\\[ \ln x=\frac{\log\_{10}x}{\log\_{10}e}. \\]

Thus, \\(\ln x\\) and \\(\log\_{10}x\\) are not interchangeable without a conversion factor.

For example,

\\[ \ln100\approx4.6052, \qquad \log\_{10}100=2. \\]

Therefore, the PNT approximation at \\(x=100\\) is

\\[ \frac{100}{\ln100}\approx21.71, \\]

not \\(100/2=50\\).

## 9.4 Probability that a large integer is prime

The textbook uses the PNT to estimate the probability that a randomly selected positive integer near a large value \\(n\\) is prime. The approximation is

\\[ \boxed{P(n\text{ is prime})\approx\frac{1}{\ln n}}. \\]

The intuition is:

- Approximately \\(n/\ln n\\) primes occur up to \\(n\\).
- There are roughly \\(n\\) positive integers up to \\(n\\).
- The estimated fraction that are prime is therefore

\\[ \frac{n/\ln n}{n}=\frac1{\ln n}. \\]

This is an approximate probability for integers near a large value, not an exact probability for every particular integer.&#x20;

### Example: An integer near \\(10^{1000}\\)

The textbook gives the example of an integer near \\(10^{1000}\\). Its approximate probability of being prime is

\\[ P\approx\frac{1}{\ln(10^{1000})}. \\]

Using logarithm laws,

\\[ \ln(10^{1000})=1000\ln10\approx2302.585. \\]

Hence,

\\[ P\approx\frac1{2302.585} \approx\frac1{2300}. \\]

So the approximate odds of a randomly chosen integer near this size being prime are about 1 in 2,300.&#x20;

### Restricting the search to odd integers

Every prime except \\(2\\) is odd. If we choose only odd integers greater than \\(2\\), we eliminate all even composite numbers from consideration.

Consequently, the chance of finding a prime in this restricted search is approximately doubled:

\\[ \boxed{P(\text{prime among selected odd integers}) \approx\frac{2}{\ln n}}. \\]

This remains an approximation; it is not an exact probability.

## 9.5 Prime density decreases as numbers increase

The PNT gives us a way to understand the density of primes among integers near a large number \\(x\\).

Approximately,

\\[ \boxed{\text{Prime density near }x\approx\frac1{\ln x}}. \\]

As \\(x\\) increases, \\(\ln x\\) increases, so \\(1/\ln x\\) decreases.

For instance:

| Approximate size \\(x\\) | \\(1/\ln x\\)    | Interpretation   |
| ------------------------ | ---------------- | ---------------- |
| \\(10^2\\)               | \\(1/4.605\\)    | About 1 in 4.6   |
| \\(10^3\\)               | \\(1/6.908\\)    | About 1 in 6.9   |
| \\(10^6\\)               | \\(1/13.816\\)   | About 1 in 13.8  |
| \\(10^{1000}\\)          | \\(1/2302.585\\) | About 1 in 2,303 |

These are asymptotic approximations. The first two rows, in particular, should not be interpreted as accurate predictions for individual small integers.

### What can we conclude?

1. Prime numbers become less frequent as a proportion of all integers.
2. The number of primes up to \\(x\\) still grows without bound.
3. The PNT does not give the exact location of the next prime.
4. A decreasing prime density does not imply that primes eventually stop appearing.

The last point is important: the existence of infinitely many primes is a separate theorem. The PNT gives much more information about their distribution.

## 9.6 Why the PNT matters

The theorem has mathematical and practical applications.

1\. Estimating prime counts

Instead of listing every prime up to a very large number, we can estimate the count using \\(x/\ln x\\).

2\. Estimating the effort needed to find large primes

If candidate integers are chosen near a large number \\(n\\), the approximate probability \\(1/\ln n\\) gives a rough indication of how many candidates might need to be examined before finding a prime.

3\. Cryptography

Large primes are important in some cryptographic systems. The PNT helps estimate how frequently prime candidates occur, although it does not itself provide a primality-testing algorithm or a prime number generator.&#x20;

### A limitation worth remembering

Trial division gives a mathematical criterion for primality, but testing every prime divisor up to \\(\sqrt n\\) becomes inefficient for very large \\(n\\). The textbook discusses more efficient primality-testing methods in the context of cryptography.&#x20;

The PNT estimates how frequently primes occur; it does not tell us whether a particular candidate is prime.

## 9.7 Common MCQ traps

- PNT formula: \\(\pi(x)\approx x/\ln x\\), not \\(x/\log\_{10}x\\).
- Exact versus approximate: \\(\pi(x)\sim x/\ln x\\) means the ratio tends to \\(1\\), not that the values are equal.
- Prime-counting notation: \\(\pi(x)\\) counts primes less than or equal to \\(x\\).
- Probability: For integers near a large \\(n\\), the approximate prime probability is \\(1/\ln n\\).
- Odd-only search: For candidates restricted to odd integers greater than \\(2\\), the approximate probability doubles.
- Density: Prime density decreases, but the total number of primes up to \\(x\\) increases without bound.
- Theorems are distinct: “There are infinitely many primes” and the Prime Number Theorem are not equivalent statements.
- Logarithm laws: \\(\ln(a^k)=k\ln a\\). In particular, \\(\ln(10^{1000})=1000\ln10\\).
- The PNT does not identify individual primes: A count estimate is not a primality test.
