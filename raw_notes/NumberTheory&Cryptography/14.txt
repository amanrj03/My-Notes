# Module 14: Computer Arithmetic with Large Integers

Number Theory-III · Rosen, Number Theory and Cryptography, Section 4.4

This module explains how an integer can be represented by its remainders modulo several smaller numbers, how arithmetic can be performed on those remainders, and how the original result can be reconstructed using the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT).

The central idea is:

Instead of working directly with a large integer, work with several smaller remainders.

This is mathematical number theory, not programming.

## 14.1 Representing an integer by its remainders

Choose pairwise relatively prime moduli \\(m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_n\\), and let

\\[ M=m_1m_2\cdots m_n. \\]

Every integer \\(a\\) in the range \\(0\le a\<M\\) can be represented by the tuple

\\[ \boxed{(a\bmod m_1,\ a\bmod m_2,\ldots,a\bmod m_n)}. \\]

Each component is simply the remainder obtained by dividing \\(a\\) by the corresponding modulus.

Rosen explains that the CRT guarantees this representation is unique for integers in that range.&#x20;

### Example: Represent integers using moduli 3 and 4

Let

\\[ m_1=3,\qquad m_2=4,\qquad M=12. \\]

Because \\(\gcd(3,4)=1\\), the standard CRT applies.

Represent \\(a=7\\):

\\[ 7\bmod3=1,\qquad 7\bmod4=3. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{7\longleftrightarrow(1,3)}. \\]

Similarly,

\\[ \begin{aligned} 0&\longleftrightarrow(0,0),\\\ 1&\longleftrightarrow(1,1),\\\ 2&\longleftrightarrow(2,2),\\\ 3&\longleftrightarrow(0,3),\\\ 4&\longleftrightarrow(1,0). \end{aligned} \\]

Every integer from \\(0\\) to \\(11\\) has a different pair of remainders.

For instance, no other integer in this range has the pair \\((1,3)\\). This uniqueness is essential: the tuple identifies the original integer.

## 14.2 Why are there exactly \\(M\\) possible representations?

For each modulus \\(m_i\\), the corresponding remainder has \\(m_i\\) possible values:

\\[ 0,1,\ldots,m_i-1. \\]

Thus, the number of possible tuples is

\\[ m_1m_2\cdots m_n=M. \\]

There are also exactly \\(M\\) integers in the range

\\[ 0,1,\ldots,M-1. \\]

The CRT ensures that every integer in this range corresponds to a unique tuple, and every valid tuple corresponds to exactly one such integer.

Exam tip: The condition that the moduli are pairwise relatively prime matters. Without it, the tuple representation need not uniquely identify every integer modulo their product.

## 14.3 Performing addition using remainder tuples

Suppose two integers \\(a\\) and \\(b\\) have representations

\\[ a\longleftrightarrow(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n), \\]

\\[ b\longleftrightarrow(b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n), \\]

where \\(a_i=a\bmod m_i\\) and \\(b_i=b\bmod m_i\\).

Their sum is represented by

\\[ \boxed{ a+b\longleftrightarrow \big((a_1+b_1)\bmod m_1,\ldots, (a_n+b_n)\bmod m_n\big). } \\]

In simple terms, add corresponding components and reduce each result using its own modulus.

### Worked example

Use the pairwise relatively prime moduli \\(5,7,11\\). Their product is

\\[ M=5\cdot7\cdot11=385. \\]

Represent \\(a=12\\) and \\(b=13\\).

For \\(12\\):

\\[ 12\bmod5=2,\quad12\bmod7=5,\quad12\bmod11=1. \\]

Thus,

\\[ 12\longleftrightarrow(2,5,1). \\]

For \\(13\\):

\\[ 13\bmod5=3,\quad13\bmod7=6,\quad13\bmod11=2. \\]

Thus,

\\[ 13\longleftrightarrow(3,6,2). \\]

Add componentwise:

\\[ \begin{aligned} (2,5,1)+(3,6,2) &=(5,11,3)\\\ &=(0,4,3), \end{aligned} \\]

where the reductions are modulo \\(5,7,11\\), respectively.

So the sum is represented by \\((0,4,3)\\). To reconstruct it, solve

\\[ x\equiv0\pmod5,\quad x\equiv4\pmod7,\quad x\equiv3\pmod{11}. \\]

The original sum is \\(12+13=25\\), and indeed

\\[ 25\bmod5=0,\quad25\bmod7=4,\quad25\bmod11=3. \\]

Therefore, the reconstructed answer is

\\[ \boxed{25}. \\]

## 14.4 Performing multiplication using remainder tuples

The same principle applies to multiplication:

\\[ \boxed{ ab\longleftrightarrow \big((a_1b_1)\bmod m_1,\ldots, (a_nb_n)\bmod m_n\big). } \\]

### Continue the same example

We have

\\[ 12\longleftrightarrow(2,5,1), \qquad 13\longleftrightarrow(3,6,2). \\]

Multiply componentwise:

\\[ \begin{aligned} (2,5,1)(3,6,2) &=(6,30,2)\\\ &=(1,2,2), \end{aligned} \\]

after reducing modulo \\(5,7,11\\).

Therefore, the product must satisfy

\\[ x\equiv1\pmod5,\quad x\equiv2\pmod7,\quad x\equiv2\pmod{11}. \\]

Since \\(12\cdot13=156\\), check:

\\[ 156\bmod5=1,\quad 156\bmod7=2,\quad 156\bmod11=2. \\]

The reconstructed result is

\\[ \boxed{156}. \\]

The calculation used only small remainders until reconstruction.

## 14.5 The crucial condition: Choose a large enough product

There is a subtle but important limitation.

For the tuple to determine the exact arithmetic result, the product of the moduli must be large enough to distinguish the possible results.

If the desired result is a nonnegative integer \\(R\\), a sufficient condition is

\\[ \boxed{0\le R\<M}. \\]

Why? The CRT identifies the result modulo \\(M\\). If \\(R\ge M\\), the tuple alone may represent \\(R\bmod M\\) rather than the exact value \\(R\\).

For example, with moduli \\(3\\) and \\(4\\), we have \\(M=12\\). The integers \\(5\\) and \\(17\\) have the same remainder tuple:

\\[ 5\longleftrightarrow(2,1), \qquad 17\longleftrightarrow(2,1). \\]

That is because \\(17\equiv5\pmod{12}\\). The tuple cannot distinguish them.

So, for exact addition of nonnegative \\(a,b\\), choose \\(M>a+b\\). For exact multiplication, choose \\(M>ab\\). More generally, choose the product of the moduli to exceed the largest possible result you need to recover. This is the condition emphasized in Rosen's discussion of computer arithmetic.&#x20;

## 14.6 Why is this useful?

Rosen identifies two main advantages of this method.&#x20;

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf



1. Handling large integers: Arithmetic can be performed on numbers much larger than the values handled efficiently by an individual processor operation.
2. Parallel computation: Calculations under different moduli can be performed independently and in parallel.

There is one trade-off: after performing the componentwise arithmetic, we may need to solve a system of congruences using CRT to recover the ordinary integer result.

The method therefore has three stages:

1\. Represent

Convert each integer into a tuple of remainders.

2\. Calculate

Perform addition or multiplication componentwise.

3\. Reconstruct

Use CRT to recover the result as an ordinary integer.

- Let \\(M=m_1m_2\cdots m_n\\).
