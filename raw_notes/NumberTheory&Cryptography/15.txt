# Module 15: Fermat’s Little Theorem

Number Theory-III · Rosen, Number Theory and Cryptography, Section 4.4

Fermat’s Little Theorem (FLT) is one of the most useful results for MCQs involving large powers modulo a prime. It lets us simplify expressions such as \\(7^{222}\bmod11\\) without calculating the enormous power.

We'll cover the theorem and its equivalent form, the conditions for applying it, worked problems, modular inverses, and the distinction between primes and pseudoprimes.

## 15.1 Statement of Fermat’s Little Theorem

Theorem: If \\(p\\) is prime and \\(a\\) is an integer not divisible by \\(p\\), then

\\[ \boxed{a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p}. \\]

The condition \\(p\nmid a\\) is essential for this form of the theorem.&#x20;

For example, because \\(11\\) is prime and \\(11\nmid7\\),

\\[ \boxed{7^{10}\equiv1\pmod{11}}. \\]

Similarly, because \\(13\\) is prime and \\(13\nmid2\\),

\\[ \boxed{2^{12}\equiv1\pmod{13}}. \\]

### The second form

Rosen also states the following form, which holds for every integer \\(a\\) whenever \\(p\\) is prime:

\\[ \boxed{a^p\equiv a\pmod p}. \\]

Unlike the first form, this version does not require \\(p\nmid a\\).

For example, with \\(a=11\\) and \\(p=11\\),

\\[ 11^{11}\equiv11\equiv0\pmod{11}. \\]

The first form cannot be applied here because \\(11\mid11\\), but the second form remains valid.

### Compare the two forms

| Form        | Statement                   | Condition                          |
| ----------- | --------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| First form  | \\(a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\\) | \\(p\\) prime and \\(p\nmid a\\)   |
| Second form | \\(a^p\equiv a\pmod p\\)    | \\(p\\) prime; any integer \\(a\\) |

MCQ trap: If \\(a\\) is divisible by \\(p\\), do not use the first form. You may still use the second form.

## 15.2 Why does the theorem simplify large powers?

Suppose \\(p\\) is prime and \\(p\nmid a\\). Fermat’s Little Theorem gives

\\[ a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p. \\]

Now write an exponent \\(n\\) using the division algorithm:

\\[ n=q(p-1)+r,\qquad 0\le r\<p-1. \\]

Then

\\[ \begin{aligned} a^n &=a^{q(p-1)+r}\\\ &=(a^{p-1})^q a^r\\\ &\equiv1^q a^r\\\ &\equiv a^r\pmod p. \end{aligned} \\]

Thus,

\\[ \boxed{a^n\equiv a^{\\,n\bmod(p-1)}\pmod p} \\]

when \\(p\\) is prime and \\(p\nmid a\\).

This means we can reduce the exponent modulo \\(p-1\\) before evaluating the power.&#x20;

## 15.3 Worked example: Find \\(7^{222}\bmod11\\)

We know \\(11\\) is prime and \\(11\nmid7\\). Therefore,

\\[ 7^{10}\equiv1\pmod{11}. \\]

Divide the exponent by \\(10\\):

\\[ 222=22(10)+2. \\]

Hence,

\\[ \begin{aligned} 7^{222} &=(7^{10})^{22}7^2\\\ &\equiv1^{22}\cdot49\pmod{11}\\\ &\equiv49\pmod{11}\\\ &\equiv5\pmod{11}. \end{aligned} \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{7^{222}\bmod11=5}. \\]

This is Rosen's worked example of applying Fermat's Little Theorem to a large exponent.&#x20;

### Another example: Find \\(2^{100}\bmod13\\)

Since \\(13\\) is prime and \\(13\nmid2\\),

\\[ 2^{12}\equiv1\pmod{13}. \\]

Divide the exponent by \\(12\\):

\\[ 100=8(12)+4. \\]

Then

\\[ \begin{aligned} 2^{100} &=(2^{12})^8 2^4\\\ &\equiv2^4\pmod{13}\\\ &\equiv16\pmod{13}\\\ &\equiv3\pmod{13}. \end{aligned} \\]

So,

\\[ \boxed{2^{100}\equiv3\pmod{13}}. \\]

### What if the exponent is a multiple of \\(p-1\\)?

If \\(p\\) is prime and \\(p\nmid a\\), then

\\[ \boxed{a^{k(p-1)}\equiv1\pmod p} \\]

for any positive integer \\(k\\).

For instance,

\\[ 3^{40}\equiv1\pmod{11}, \\]

because \\(40=4(10)\\) and \\(3^{10}\equiv1\pmod{11}\\).

## 15.4 Using Fermat’s Little Theorem to find modular inverses

Recall from Module 12 that an inverse of \\(a\\) modulo \\(m\\) satisfies

\\[ aa^{-1}\equiv1\pmod m. \\]

When \\(p\\) is prime and \\(p\nmid a\\), FLT gives

\\[ a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p. \\]

Rewrite this as

\\[ a\cdot a^{p-2}\equiv1\pmod p. \\]

Therefore, \\(a^{p-2}\\) is an inverse of \\(a\\) modulo \\(p\\):

\\[ \boxed{a^{-1}\equiv a^{p-2}\pmod p}. \\]

### Example: Find the inverse of \\(5\\) modulo \\(41\\)

Since \\(41\\) is prime and \\(41\nmid5\\),

\\[ 5^{-1}\equiv5^{39}\pmod{41}. \\]

We can verify the inverse using repeated squaring:

\\[ 5^2=25, \\]

\\[ 5^4\equiv25^2=625\equiv10\pmod{41}, \\]

\\[ 5^8\equiv10^2=100\equiv18\pmod{41}, \\]

\\[ 5^{16}\equiv18^2=324\equiv37\equiv-4\pmod{41}, \\]

\\[ 5^{32}\equiv(-4)^2=16\pmod{41}. \\]

Since \\(39=32+4+2+1\\),

\\[ \begin{aligned} 5^{39} &=5^{32}5^4 5^2 5\\\ &\equiv16\cdot10\cdot25\cdot5\pmod{41}\\\ &\equiv33\pmod{41}. \end{aligned} \\]

Check:

\\[ 5(33)=165=4(41)+1. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{5^{-1}\equiv33\pmod{41}}. \\]

When to use which method: The Euclidean algorithm works for any relatively prime coefficient and modulus. The FLT method is available when the modulus is prime and the coefficient is not divisible by that prime.

## 15.5 A critical limitation: The modulus must be prime

You cannot blindly apply Fermat’s Little Theorem to a composite modulus.

For example, consider \\(a=2\\) and modulus \\(9\\). It would be incorrect to conclude that

\\[ 2^8\equiv1\pmod9 \\]

just because \\(8=9-1\\).

In fact,

\\[ 2^8=256\equiv4\pmod9. \\]

The theorem's prime-modulus condition is essential.

A related trap: even when the modulus is prime, the first form requires that \\(a\\) not be divisible by \\(p\\). If \\(p\mid a\\), then \\(a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\\) is generally false.

## 15.6 The converse is not always true: Pseudoprimes

Fermat's Little Theorem says that if \\(p\\) is prime and \\(p\nmid a\\), then

\\[ a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p. \\]

A natural question is: If an integer \\(n\\) satisfies \\(a^{n-1}\equiv1\pmod n\\), must \\(n\\) be prime?

No. The converse is false.

A pseudoprime to base \\(a\\) is a composite integer \\(n\\) satisfying

\\[ \boxed{a^{n-1}\equiv1\pmod n}. \\]

The base must be relatively prime to \\(n\\) for this standard pseudoprime test.

### Example: \\(341\\) is a pseudoprime to base \\(2\\)

First, factor it:

\\[ 341=11\cdot31. \\]

So \\(341\\) is composite. Now observe that

\\[ 2^{10}=1024=3(341)+1, \\]

which means

\\[ 2^{10}\equiv1\pmod{341}. \\]

Since \\(340=34(10)\\),

\\[ \begin{aligned} 2^{340} &=(2^{10})^{34}\\\ &\equiv1^{34}\\\ &\equiv1\pmod{341}. \end{aligned} \\]

Thus, \\(341\\) passes the Fermat test for base \\(2\\), even though it is composite.

Conclusion: Passing a single Fermat test does not prove that a number is prime.

## 15.7 Carmichael numbers

Rosen introduces a stronger type of counterexample: a Carmichael number is a composite integer \\(n\\) satisfying

\\[ \boxed{a^{n-1}\equiv1\pmod n} \\]

for every positive integer \\(a\\) relatively prime to \\(n\\).&#x20;

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf



The textbook's example is

\\[ \boxed{561=3\cdot11\cdot17}. \\]

It is composite, but it satisfies the Fermat congruence for every base relatively prime to \\(561\\).

The distinction is:

| Type                        | What it satisfies                                                           |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pseudoprime to base \\(a\\) | Passes the Fermat congruence for a particular base \\(a\\)                  |
| Carmichael number           | Passes the Fermat congruence for every positive base relatively prime to it |

For MCQs, remember that a Carmichael number is composite. It demonstrates why Fermat's Little Theorem gives a necessary property of primes, but not a sufficient test for primality.

## 15.8 Common MCQ traps

- Trap 1: The modulus \\(p\\) must be prime to apply Fermat's Little Theorem.
- Trap 2: \\(a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\\) requires \\(p\nmid a\\).
- Trap 3: \\(a^p\equiv a\pmod p\\) holds for every integer \\(a\\) when \\(p\\) is prime.
- Trap 4: When reducing the exponent, use \\(p-1\\), not \\(p\\), in the first form.
- Trap 5: The inverse of \\(a\\) modulo prime \\(p\\) is \\(a^{p-2}\bmod p\\), provided \\(p\nmid a\\).
- Trap 6: A composite number can pass the Fermat test for some bases.
- Trap 7: A Carmichael number passes the Fermat congruence for every base relatively prime to it.
