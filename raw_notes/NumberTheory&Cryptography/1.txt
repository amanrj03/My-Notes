## 1. Divisibility of integers

### 1.1 Definition and notation

For integers \\(a,b\\), with \\(a\ne 0\\), we say that \\(a\\) divides \\(b\\) if there exists an integer \\(k\\) such that

\\[ b=ak,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z. \\]

We write \\(a\mid b\\). If no such integer \\(k\\) exists, we write \\(a\nmid b\\).

The integer \\(a\\) is a divisor or factor of \\(b\\), while \\(b\\) is a multiple of \\(a\\).

Important: Divisibility is a statement about integers, not about whether a quotient exists in the real numbers. For example,

\\[ 3\mid 12,\qquad 3\nmid 14, \\]

because \\(12/3=4\\) is an integer, whereas \\(14/3\\) is not.

### 1.2 Conditions that MCQs frequently test

- The divisor \\(a\\) must be nonzero under the textbook's definition.
- The dividend \\(b\\) may be positive, negative, or zero.
- The multiplier \\(k\\) must be an integer; it need not be positive.
- Divisibility does not require \\(b/a\\) to be positive.
- Every nonzero integer divides zero, because \\(0=a\cdot0\\).
- \\(1\\) and \\(-1\\) divide every integer.

For instance,

\\[ -4\mid 20,\qquad 4\mid -20,\qquad 7\mid 0. \\]

All three are true.

But \\(0\mid 7\\) is not defined under this definition. Also, \\(0\mid0\\) is not allowed under it, even though the equation \\(0=0\cdot k\\) holds for every integer \\(k\\).

Do not confuse the definition with the separate question of whether \\(0\\) divides another integer.

## 2. Fundamental properties of divisibility

Your textbook states the following three properties for integers \\(a,b,c\\), where \\(a\ne0\\).&#x20;

### Property 1: Sum property

If \\(a\mid b\\) and \\(a\mid c\\), then

\\[ \boxed{a\mid(b+c)}. \\]

The proof follows directly from the definition. Suppose

\\[ b=ax,\qquad c=ay \\]

for integers \\(x,y\\). Then

\\[ b+c=a(x+y). \\]

Since \\(x+y\\) is an integer, \\(a\mid(b+c)\\).

The same reasoning also gives

\\[ a\mid(b-c). \\]

MCQ consequence: If a number divides two integers, it divides their sum and difference.

### Property 2: Multiplication property

If \\(a\mid b\\), then for every integer \\(c\\),

\\[ \boxed{a\mid bc}. \\]

Indeed, if \\(b=ax\\), then \\(bc=a(xc)\\), and \\(xc\\) is an integer.

This remains true when \\(c=0\\) or \\(c\\) is negative.

### Property 3: Transitivity

If

\\[ a\mid b \quad\text{and}\quad b\mid c, \\]

then

\\[ \boxed{a\mid c}. \\]

To prove this, write \\(b=ax\\) and \\(c=by\\), with \\(x,y\in\mathbb Z\\). Substituting,

\\[ c=(ax)y=a(xy). \\]

Since \\(xy\\) is an integer, \\(a\mid c\\).

### 2.1 Linear-combination property

A particularly useful consequence is

\\[ \boxed{a\mid b,\ a\mid c \implies a\mid (mb+nc)} \\]

for all integers \\(m,n\\).

This includes sums, differences, and arbitrary integer multiples.

For example, if \\(7\mid 35\\) and \\(7\mid 21\\), then

\\[ 7\mid(2\cdot35-3\cdot21)=7. \\]

The converse is not generally valid: knowing that \\(a\mid(mb+nc)\\) does not automatically imply that \\(a\mid b\\) and \\(a\mid c\\).

### 2.2 More divisibility results worth memorizing

These are natural consequences of the definition and are useful in statement-based MCQs.

Result A — Mutual divisibility

For nonzero integers \\(a,b\\),

\\[ a\mid b\ \text{and}\ b\mid a \iff \boxed{|a|=|b|}. \\]

Equivalently, \\(a=b\\) or \\(a=-b\\).

Result B — Divisibility bounds

If \\(a\mid b\\), with \\(a\ne0\\) and \\(b\ne0\\), then

\\[ \boxed{|a|\le |b|}. \\]

Why? Since \\(b=ak\\), the integer \\(k\\) is nonzero, so \\(|k|\ge1\\). Therefore \\(|b|=|a||k|\ge|a|\\).

Result C — Counting positive multiples

For positive integers \\(n,d\\), the number of positive integers not exceeding \\(n\\) that are divisible by \\(d\\) is

\\[ \boxed{\left\lfloor\frac nd\right\rfloor}. \\]

For example, the positive multiples of \\(6\\) not exceeding \\(25\\) are \\(6,12,18,24\\). Their number is

\\[ \left\lfloor\frac{25}{6}\right\rfloor=4. \\]

The floor function means the greatest integer less than or equal to its argument.

Result D — A common false statement

If \\(a\mid bc\\), it does not necessarily follow that \\(a\mid b\\) or \\(a\mid c\\).

Counterexample:

\\[ 6\mid(2\cdot3),\qquad 6\nmid2,\qquad 6\nmid3. \\]

The stronger conclusion becomes valid when \\(a\\) is prime; we will study that property in Number Theory-II.

## 3. The Division Algorithm

This is one of the most important results in Module 1, especially because MCQs frequently use negative dividends.

### 3.1 Exact theorem

Let \\(a\\) be any integer and \\(d\\) a positive integer. Then there exist unique integers \\(q,r\\) such that

\\[ \boxed{a=dq+r,\qquad 0\le r\<d}. \\]

Here:

- \\(a\\): dividend
- \\(d\\): positive divisor
- \\(q\\): quotient
- \\(r\\): remainder

The conditions are essential. The remainder must be nonnegative and strictly less than the divisor.

Notice that the theorem allows \\(a\\) to be negative, but requires \\(d>0\\).

### 3.2 Existence and uniqueness

There are two distinct claims in the theorem.

Existence: At least one pair \\((q,r)\\) satisfies the equation and remainder bounds.

Uniqueness: No two different pairs satisfy all those conditions.

To understand uniqueness, suppose

\\[ a=dq+r=dq'+r', \\]

where \\(0\le r,r'\<d\\).

Subtracting,

\\[ d(q-q')=r'-r. \\]

Because both remainders lie between \\(0\\) and \\(d-1\\),

\\[ -(d-1)\le r'-r\le d-1. \\]

The difference \\(r'-r\\) is a multiple of \\(d\\), but the only multiple of \\(d\\) strictly between \\(-d\\) and \\(d\\) is zero. Therefore,

\\[ r'=r,\qquad q'=q. \\]

Thus the quotient and remainder are unique.

MCQ trap: The equation \\(a=dq+r\\) alone is insufficient to determine the quotient and remainder uniquely. The bounds \\(0\le r\<d\\) are part of the theorem.

### 3.3 Quotient and remainder notation

Your textbook uses

\\[ \boxed{q=a\operatorname{ div}d,\qquad r=a\operatorname{ mod}d}. \\]

For positive \\(d\\), the quotient and remainder can be calculated using the floor function:

\\[ \boxed{a\operatorname{ div}d= \left\lfloor\frac ad\right\rfloor} \\]

and

\\[ \boxed{a\operatorname{ mod}d= a-d\left\lfloor\frac ad\right\rfloor}. \\]

The second formula follows by rearranging \\(a=dq+r\\).

The floor, not ordinary rounding or truncation toward zero, is crucial when \\(a\\) is negative.

## 4. Negative dividends: the most important trap

Consider dividing \\(-11\\) by \\(3\\).

We need integers \\(q,r\\) satisfying

\\[ -11=3q+r,\qquad 0\le r<3. \\]

The correct representation is

\\[ \boxed{-11=3(-4)+1}. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{-11\operatorname{ div}3=-4,\qquad -11\operatorname{ mod}3=1}. \\]

Why not quotient \\(-3\\) and remainder \\(-2\\)?

Although

\\[ -11=3(-3)-2, \\]

the remainder \\(-2\\) violates \\(0\le r<3\\).

For any real number \\(x\\),

- \\(\lfloor x\rfloor\\) is the greatest integer less than or equal to \\(x\\).
- Truncation toward zero removes the fractional part, moving toward zero.

For negative nonintegers, these operations differ.

### 4.2 Worked examples

| Dividend \\(a\\) | Divisor \\(d\\) | Quotient \\(q\\) | Remainder \\(r\\) |
| ---------------- | --------------- | ---------------- | ----------------- |
| \\(17\\)         | \\(5\\)         | \\(3\\)          | \\(2\\)           |
| \\(-17\\)        | \\(5\\)         | \\(-4\\)         | \\(3\\)           |
| \\(17\\)         | \\(5\\)         | \\(3\\)          | \\(2\\)           |

| \\(0\\)          | \\(5\\)         | \\(0\\)          | \\(0\\)           |

The repeated positive example reinforces the rule; the negative examples are where mistakes most often occur.

For \\(-17\\) divided by \\(5\\),

\\[ -17=5(-4)+3, \\]

and for \\(-1\\) divided by \\(3\\),

\\[ -1=3(-1)+2. \\]

In both cases, the remainder is in \\(\\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\\}\\).

### 4.3 General shortcut

For any integer \\(a\\) and positive integer \\(d\\),

\\[ q=\left\lfloor\frac ad\right\rfloor,\qquad r=a-dq. \\]

Use these formulas even if the dividend is negative. They work for every integer dividend.

Divisibility connection: An integer \\(a\\) is divisible by a positive integer \\(d\\) exactly when

\\[ \boxed{a\operatorname{ mod}d=0}. \\]

This equivalence will become particularly useful in modular arithmetic.

## 5. Deeper consequences of the Division Algorithm

### 5.1 Every remainder belongs to a fixed set

For a fixed positive divisor \\(d\\), every integer has exactly one remainder among

\\[ \boxed{0,1,2,\ldots,d-1}. \\]

There are exactly \\(d\\) possible remainders.

For example, division by \\(7\\) can produce only \\(0,1,2,3,4,5,6\\). A remainder of \\(7\\) or \\(-1\\) is impossible under the theorem.

This remains true for negative dividends.

### 5.2 When the remainder is zero

From

\\[ a=dq+r,\qquad 0\le r\<d, \\]

we get

\\[ \boxed{d\mid a\iff r=0}. \\]

The forward direction follows because if \\(d\mid a\\), then \\(a=dq\\) for some integer \\(q\\), so the remainder is zero. Conversely, if \\(r=0\\), then \\(a=dq\\), which means \\(d\mid a\\).

### 5.3 Quotient and remainder are functions

For fixed \\(d>0\\), both \\(a\operatorname{ div}d\\) and \\(a\operatorname{ mod}d\\) assign exactly one integer to each integer \\(a\\).

However, they behave differently:

- The quotient can be any integer.
- The remainder always lies between \\(0\\) and \\(d-1\\).
- The quotient changes as \\(a\\) changes.
- The remainder repeats periodically with period \\(d\\).

For example,

\\[ 8\operatorname{ mod}5 =13\operatorname{ mod}5 =18\operatorname{ mod}5 =3. \\]

Adding \\(d\\) to a dividend preserves its remainder:

\\[ \boxed{(a+d)\operatorname{ mod}d=a\operatorname{ mod}d}. \\]

More generally, adding any integer multiple of \\(d\\) preserves the remainder.

### 5.4 Quotient formula for positive integers

For positive integers \\(n,k\\), the textbook gives the identity

\\[ \boxed{ \left\lfloor\frac nk\right\rfloor = \left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{k}\right\rfloor+1 }. \\]

This identity is useful for counting multiples and converting between integer bounds. It applies when \\(n,k\\) are positive integers.

For example,

\\[ \left\lfloor\frac{20}{6}\right\rfloor = \left\lfloor\frac{19}{6}\right\rfloor+1 =3+1=4. \\]

### 5.5 Smallest-absolute-value representative

A related congruence question asks for the integer of smallest absolute value congruent to \\(a\\) modulo \\(m\\).

Since all such integers differ by multiples of \\(m\\), a representative can be chosen from the interval centred at zero. For odd \\(m\\), the usual interval is

\\[ -\frac{m-1}{2}\le r\le\frac{m-1}{2}. \\]

For even \\(m\\), the two integers \\(m/2\\) and \\(-m/2\\) have equal absolute value and are congruent modulo \\(m\\). Thus the smallest-absolute-value representative need not be unique.

Example: modulo \\(9\\), the representative of \\(20\\) is \\(2\\), since \\(20=2+2(9)\\). The representative of \\(17\\) is \\(-1\\), since \\(17=-1+2(9)\\).

We will formalize congruence classes in Module 2.
