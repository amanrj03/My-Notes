# Module 11: GCD as a Linear Combination

Number Theory-II · Rosen, Number Theory and Cryptography, Section 4.3

This module connects the Euclidean algorithm to one of the most important results in number theory: Bézout's theorem.

Previously, we used repeated division to calculate a GCD. Now we'll learn how to express that GCD in the form

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(a,b)=sa+tb} \\]

where \\(s\\) and \\(t\\) are integers.

This is more than a calculation trick. It establishes a mathematical property of the GCD and becomes useful later when solving linear congruences and finding modular inverses.

## 11.1 What is a linear combination?

A linear combination of integers \\(a\\) and \\(b\\) is an expression of the form

\\[ \boxed{sa+tb} \\]

where \\(s,t\in\mathbb Z\\).

The coefficients may be positive, negative, or zero.

For example, let \\(a=12\\) and \\(b=8\\). Then

\\[ 2(12)-1(8)=16 \\]

is a linear combination of \\(12\\) and \\(8\\).

Likewise,

\\[ 1(12)-1(8)=4 \\]

is another linear combination.

Since

\\[ \gcd(12,8)=4, \\]

we have expressed their GCD as a linear combination:

\\[ \boxed{4=1(12)-1(8)}. \\]

The key question is: Can we always express the GCD of two integers as a linear combination of them?

The answer is yes, provided they are not both zero.

## 11.2 Bézout's theorem

Theorem: If \\(a\\) and \\(b\\) are positive integers, there exist integers \\(s\\) and \\(t\\) such that

\\[ \boxed{\gcd(a,b)=sa+tb}. \\]

This is Bézout's theorem, and the equation is called Bézout's identity. The integers \\(s,t\\) are called Bézout coefficients.&#x20;

### Example: \\(\gcd(6,14)=2\\)

We can write

\\[ \boxed{2=(-2)(6)+(1)(14)}. \\]

Here,

\\[ s=-2,\qquad t=1. \\]

Verify:

\\[ -12+14=2. \\]

The negative coefficient is perfectly valid. Bézout coefficients do not have to be positive.

### What the theorem does not say

Bézout's theorem does not claim that every linear combination \\(sa+tb\\) equals the GCD. It says that at least one choice of integer coefficients produces the GCD.

For example, \\(2(6)+3(14)=54\\), which is a linear combination but not the GCD.

## 11.3 Finding Bézout coefficients by reversing the Euclidean algorithm

The textbook's method is to first calculate the GCD using successive divisions, then work backward to express the last nonzero remainder in terms of the original numbers.&#x20;

MTH381 U123 TB.pdf



Let's work through the full process.

### Example: Express \\(\gcd(252,198)=18\\) as a linear combination

Step 1: Apply the Euclidean algorithm.

\\[ 252=1(198)+54 \tag{1} \\]

\\[ 198=3(54)+36 \tag{2} \\]

\\[ 54=1(36)+18 \tag{3} \\]

\\[ 36=2(18)+0 \tag{4} \\]

The last nonzero remainder is \\(18\\). Thus,

\\[ \gcd(252,198)=18. \\]

Step 2: Start with the equation containing the GCD.

From equation (3),

\\[ 18=54-36. \\]

This expresses \\(18\\) as a linear combination of \\(54\\) and \\(36\\).

Step 3: Replace \\(36\\) using equation (2).

From equation (2),

\\[ 36=198-3(54). \\]

Substitute:

\\[ \begin{aligned} 18&=54-[198-3(54)]\\\ &=54-198+3(54)\\\ &=4(54)-198. \end{aligned} \\]

Now \\(18\\) is a linear combination of \\(54\\) and \\(198\\).

Step 4: Replace \\(54\\) using equation (1).

From equation (1),

\\[ 54=252-198. \\]

Substitute:

\\[ \begin{aligned} 18&=4(252-198)-198\\\ &=4(252)-4(198)-198\\\ &=4(252)-5(198). \end{aligned} \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{18=4(252)-5(198)}. \\]

The Bézout coefficients are

\\[ \boxed{s=4,\qquad t=-5}. \\]

This is the worked example given in your textbook.&#x20;

## 11.4 Why does reversing the divisions work?

Every division in the Euclidean algorithm has the form

\\[ r=a-bq. \\]

Thus, each remainder is an integer linear combination of the two numbers used in that division.

For example,

\\[ 54=252-198. \\]

So \\(54\\) is a linear combination of the original inputs. Similarly,

\\[ 36=198-3(54) \\]

is also a linear combination of \\(252\\) and \\(198\\), because \\(54\\) already is one.

Repeating this reasoning backward shows that the last nonzero remainder—the GCD—is an integer linear combination of the original inputs.

This explains the construction behind Bézout's identity. Your textbook states Bézout's theorem in Section 4.3 and refers to a later section for a formal proof.&#x20;

## 11.5 Are Bézout coefficients unique?

No. Different integer pairs can represent the same GCD.

For example, since

\\[ 2=(-2)(6)+14, \\]

we have one representation of \\(\gcd(6,14)=2\\).

But also,

\\[ 2=5(6)-2(14)=30-28. \\]

Both are valid.

More generally, if

\\[ sa+tb=d,\qquad d=\gcd(a,b), \\]

then for any integer \\(k\\),

\\[ \boxed{ (s+kb)a+(t-ka)b=d }. \\]

Why? Expanding the expression gives

\\[ sa+kab+tb-kab=sa+tb=d. \\]

The extra terms cancel.

Therefore, Bézout coefficients are generally not unique.

### A useful special case

If \\(\gcd(a,b)=1\\), Bézout's identity becomes

\\[ \boxed{sa+tb=1}. \\]

For example, because \\(\gcd(3,7)=1\\),

\\[ \boxed{(-2)(3)+(1)(7)=1}. \\]

This particular form will be essential in Module 12 when we study modular inverses.

## 11.6 A key consequence: the GCD divides every linear combination

Let

\\[ d=\gcd(a,b). \\]

Since \\(d\mid a\\) and \\(d\mid b\\), it follows that

\\[ d\mid sa \quad\text{and}\quad d\mid tb \\]

for all integers \\(s,t\\). Hence,

\\[ \boxed{d\mid(sa+tb)}. \\]

In plain language, every integer linear combination of \\(a\\) and \\(b\\) is divisible by their GCD.

For example, every expression \\(6s+14t\\), with integer \\(s,t\\), must be even because \\(\gcd(6,14)=2\\).

This gives a useful test:

- If \\(sa+tb=1\\) for some integers \\(s,t\\), then \\(\gcd(a,b)=1\\).
- If \\(\gcd(a,b)=1\\), Bézout's theorem guarantees that such integers \\(s,t\\) exist.

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b)=1 \iff \exists s,t\in\mathbb Z:\ sa+tb=1 }. \\]

This equivalence is one of the most important results to remember for MCQs.
